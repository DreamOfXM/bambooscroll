Cut Back the Fiefs

Wu, Liang and Chang'an, 154 BCE — A minister proposed cutting the great fiefs back and was cut first, still in his court robes, in a marketplace; seven kings rose anyway, and were broken in three months by a commander who would not give battle and would not obey an order to.

This episode is told from the official histories: the memoir of the king of Wu and the hereditary house of the Marquis of Jiang in the Records of the Grand Historian, its memoirs of Yuan Ang and Chao Cuo and of Han Changru, its annals of Emperor Jing; the Book of Han's annals of Emperor Jing and its own biographies of the same men; and the Zizhi Tongjian, which lays the three out in one year. Nothing from a novel or a stage play is a source for a panel; what those traditions add is dealt with at the end, in the Myth Check.
On our sources
02 / 18 Commentary
Liu Zhong, enfeoffed as king of Dai, abandoned his kingdom in 200 BCE and rode off alone to Luoyang to give himself up; the Shiji says the emperor, for the sake of the flesh, would not apply the law and deposed him to a marquisate. His son Liu Pi had served as a cavalry general under the emperor in 196 and been made king of Wu because the men of Wu and Kuaiji were swift and unruly and needed a strong man to hold them. The Records put one more scene in the same creation: the emperor looked at his nephew's face, said he had the look of a rebel, regretted the words after the seal was struck, clapped him on the back and told him not to rise: within fifty years of the Han there will be trouble in the south-east, he said, and is it not you? The war came forty-two years after the enfeoffment, which is one reason to read the prophecy as a story told afterwards.
Shiji, juan 106, Memoir of the King of Wu; Shiji, juan 93; Hanshu, juan 1, Annals of Emperor Gaozu
03 / 18 In the sources
The record of Wu's standing is one sentence in the king's own memoir: Wu had the copper hills of Yuzhang commandery, and Pi gathered the empire's runaways to mine them and coin the ore, and boiled the sea for salt, and for that reason took no land tax from his people, so that his expenditure was inexhaustible. When a man owed service he was paid the market rate of the substitute rather than the usual reckoning; the kingdom's talented men and its neighbourhoods were visited and given presents each season; and officials from other kingdoms who came to seize runaways did not get them. The Shiji says this went on for forty-odd years, and draws the conclusion itself: that is why he was able to set their multitude to work.
Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 35, Memoir of Jing, Yan and the King of Wu
04 / 18 In the sources
The Wu crown prince was sent back to be buried at home; the king of Wu sent him back to Chang'an — if we are of the same line, when a man dies in Chang'an he is buried in Chang'an. Gaozu's grandson then pleaded illness and stopped attending court. The capital knew the illness was about the boy, tested it, arrested and interrogated several parties of Wu envoys; only then did the king send a substitute to make the autumn court-call, and the envoy's answer is on the record: the king was really not ill, but began by pretending, was caught out, was pressed, and now has nothing left to plan except fear — and he closes on a proverb: to see the fish in the deep pool is not a lucky thing. The emperor pardoned the envoys, sent a staff and a seat — the old man's mark — and excused the attendance. Sima Qian's own verdict on the whole affair puts the origin in that game: the contest for a trick started the trouble, he writes, and in the end it destroyed the root.
Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 35
05 / 18 In the sources
Chao Cuo's memorial is preserved: the empire had enfeoffed the illegitimate branches of the imperial family so generously that the king of Qi held seventy-odd cities, the king of Chu forty-odd and the king of Wu fifty-odd; three such branches of the house, he says, divided half the empire between them. He had also said, of Wu's minting and salt-boiling and its recruiting of the desperate, that the time for indulgence was over. The man himself is characterised in one line in his own biography: severe, upright, and hard on others; as crown prince's household officer he was so persuasive that the eastern palace called him the bag of wit. His own faction's count of what he drafted is thirty chapters of new enactments, and the note on their effect is that the lords went loud about them.
Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 49, Memoir of Yuan Ang and Chao Cuo
06 / 18 In the sources
Chao Cuo's father came on from Yingchuan to ask why, and the dialogue is recorded in both histories: the house of Liu is safe, but the house of Chao is in danger — I am leaving you and going home. He took poison and said he could not bear to watch the calamity reach his own body. The Shiji and the Hanshu tell the episode in the same order and with the same interval: the father poisoned himself, and a little over ten days later the seven kingdoms rose, nominally to kill him. What the two books agree on is that his own household saw the price before the emperor did.
Shiji, juan 101, Memoir of Yuan Ang and Chao Cuo; Hanshu, juan 49; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 16
07 / 18 In the sources
Ying Gao's pitch to the king of Jiaoxi is quoted at length: Wu and Jiaoxi are princes of reputation and will be inspected together; the king of Wu has not dared attend court for twenty years and has to keep his shoulders shrugged and his feet together; and the thing being aimed at is not the cutting of land. The king of Jiaoxi said yes, then went and booked the rest of the eastern kingdoms in a chain. The record of who actually moved is precise. The emperor's annal names the seven: Wu, Chu, Zhao, Jiaoxi, Jinan, Zichuan and Jiaodong. The king of Qi regretted the pact and poisoned himself; the king of Jibei was seized and held by his own senior secretary and could not raise a man; the three Qi kingdoms put their hope in Jiaoxi as their lead and together sat down before Linzi; and the king of Zhao sent north to the Xiongnu to join hands.
Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 5, Annals of Emperor Jing
08 / 18 In the sources
The Shiji gives the levy as two hundred thousand and odd men, and his letter to the other kings as a claim of five hundred thousand picked troops in Wu plus three hundred thousand more from the south, with a price-list on the front: five thousand catties of gold and a ten-thousand-household marquisate for a general's head, coming down to five hundred catties and five hundred households for an officer of the rank of a thousand shi, and every town or army that came in over counted as so many heads. Wu's own roll of invitations ran to the kings of Huainan, Hengshan and Lujiang and the son of the old king of Changsha — none of whom rose. Every one of these numbers is a claim in a source, not a return.
Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 35
09 / 18 In the sources
Yuan Ang's line was that Wu had the profit of copper and salt, and nothing else: had it really gathered able men they would be helping the king to act properly, not to rise. The emperor then asked what was to be done; Ang asked for the room to be cleared, and when only Chao Cuo was left, said that what he had to say was not for a minister's ears. Cuo went out to the east of the partition, and the record notes that he resented it extremely. The proposal was the rebels' own programme turned against them: kill Cuo, pardon the seven, give back the cut land, and the armies would go home without bloodshed. The emperor was silent a long time before answering.
Shiji, juan 106; Shiji, juan 101; Hanshu, juan 49
10 / 18 In the sources
The indictment is preserved and it is worth reading as a document: the chancellor, the Commandant of the Capital and the Commandant of Justice accuse Cuo of wanting the emperor to lead out in person while he kept the capital himself, of proposing to hand Wu the towns of Xu and Tong that it had not yet taken, and therefore of having no minister's manners — sentence: waist-cutting, and the execution of his parents, wife, children and same-born brothers, old and young, in the market. The emperor's reply is one word: approved. The Han annal of the reign does the whole business in one clause: the Censor-in-chief Chao Cuo was cut down, to apologise to the seven kingdoms. The Shiji's annal of Emperor Jing adds the outcome just as flatly: the envoy was sent to announce the pardon, and it did not stop them; the rebels went on west and invested Liang.
Shiji, juan 101; Hanshu, juan 49; Hanshu, juan 5, Annals of Emperor Jing
11 / 18 In the sources
The man who got Yuan Ang out was the clerk who had once slept with his maid: Ang had said nothing, let the man run, and given him the girl; the clerk had come back into service and was now a squadron commander in the unit detailed to guard him. He spent his whole kit on two dan jars of strong wine, got the guard at the southwest corner of the perimeter drunk, cut the tent with a knife and walked Ang out between the sleeping men. Ang left his credential and its tufts in his bosom, went on foot seventy li in wooden clogs, saw Liang horse at daylight and rode away to report. What the king of Wu's court had wanted from him is in the same memoir: to make him a general. The Shiji and Hanshu agree on the line he is reported to have given instead.
Shiji, juan 101; Hanshu, juan 49
12 / 18 In the sources
Deng Gong's other sentence is the one that stung: I fear the empire's able men will stop speaking now. Cuo's plan was the ten-thousand-generation benefit of the capital's supremacy; it had begun to be carried out and its author was destroyed — inside, you have closed the mouths of the loyal; outside, you have avenged the lords for them. The emperor let out a long breath and said the sentence the record keeps as his own verdict on his own decision: your words are right, and I regret it too. He made Deng Gong a commandery military commandant, and the war went on.
Shiji, juan 101; Hanshu, juan 49
13 / 18 In the sources
The strategy was agreed in advance, in those words, and the emperor approved it. The detour over the Blue Fields and out of Wu Pass came from a man named Zhao She who stopped the column at Bashang and warned that Wu would have killers laid in the defiles of Yao and Mian; the search found them, and Yafu asked that the man be made a protector of the army. At Luoyang he got hold of Ju Meng, a knight of the capital region, and said what the record preserves: seven kingdoms have risen and I have reached here alive, which I did not expect; I also thought the lords had got Ju Meng, and he has not stirred; I will hold Xingyang and there is nothing to worry about east of here. His father's old retainer at Huaiyang supplied the rest of the plan: deep trenches, high walls, and light troops at the Huai-Si mouth. He had been marked out by the late emperor at Xiliu, where the gate-captain had told the imperial advance party — in this army we hear the general's orders, not the Son of Heaven's edict.
Shiji, juan 57, Hereditary House of the Marquis of Jiang; Hanshu, juan 40; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 16
14 / 18 Debated
The order of battle in the emperor's annal is four commands: the Grand Marshal Yafu with thirty-six general's titles against Wu and Chu, the Marquis of Quzhou against Zhao, the general Luan Bu against Qi, and the Grand General Dou Ying at Xingyang to watch the east. Wu and Chu's first blow fell on Liang — an army sent out to meet them was defeated, two Liang generals were broken, and the king shut himself in Suiyang. On the first week of the war the three accounts do not agree: the Shiji says the king of Liang sent out six generals and lost two of them, the Hanshu says he sent out generals and lost two armies, and the Tongjian adds a body count of tens of thousands at Jibi that neither earlier book carries. Liang then made its own war useful: Han Anguo and Zhang Yu were made generals, of whom the record says the one fought hard and the one held steady, so that Wu could not get past Liang. Behind the front, Yafu's Marquis of Gongguo and the light horse cut the road the grain came by. The memoir marks the beginning of the quarrel that killed the commander with one flat clause: from this point the king of Liang and the Commandant were at odds.
Hanshu, juan 5; Shiji, juan 57; Shiji, juan 108, Memoir of Han Changru; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 16
15 / 18 In the sources
Tian Lubo's column was to take Hainan and Changsha and join him at Wu Pass; the crown prince's objection was that a rebellion cannot lend out soldiers, because lent soldiers turn on you. The young commander Huan's argument is the sharpest thing either side said: Wu has infantry, and infantry wants rough ground; Han has chariot and horse, and horse wants open country. If you go slowly and stop to take the cities, the Han cavalry will reach the open ground between Liang and Chu and it is finished. The old generals overruled him: this is what a boy with a sharp point calls strategy. The record then notes that Wu did besiege the cities, and that the king's guests were all made generals, commandants and majors, and the emperor's own edict afterwards tells what that cost the people of the east: they burned the ancestral temples, seized the imperial equipage and dug up graves.
Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 35
16 / 18 In the sources
Then the move that reads like a formula from a book of war, recorded three times over: the rebels went to the south-east corner of the rampart, Yafu had his men hold the north-west, and the picked troops came at the north-west and could not get in. The host was eating up its own grain by then. Yafu sent his best men out after them and broke them at Xiayi; the emperor's annal gives the result as a hundred thousand and odd heads taken, which is a claim in a source and also a count of men who mostly died of hunger. The edict issued to the generals at the same moment is the plainest statement of what was wanted: strike deep and kill a great deal, and anyone taken who ranks at three hundred shi and above is to be killed; no prisoners; anyone who argues with this edict is to be cut at the waist.
Shiji, juan 57; Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 5
17 / 18 Debated
The end is recorded as a transaction: the Yue lured him out — he came to review their force — and a man struck him down, and his head went off by courier carriage. His sons went to the Minyue, and the grudge there outlived him. The two histories differ slightly on the flight: the Shiji has him cross the Yangzi with several thousand, the Hanshu cross the Huai with a thousand; both have Dantu, and both have the men of Yue cutting off the king of Wu's head. Of the rest of the seven: the king of Chu, defeated, killed himself; the kings of Jiaoxi, Jiaodong, Zichuan and Jinan died and their kingdoms were absorbed; Zhao held Handan for ten months and its king killed himself when Wu and Chu were gone. The emperor's annal names all six of them in one sentence and says of them plainly that they killed themselves.
Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 35; Hanshu, juan 5
18 / 18 In the sources
The Marquis of Gongguo received the king of Jiaoxi with the gold and drum of his office and asked him, in the record's polite form, to explain why he had mobilised; and then read him the edict and told him to make up his own mind. The sentence that sums up the war's politics is in the same exchange: if Cuo really was a bad minister, why did you not report him — instead you raised troops without tally or order and struck at a loyal kingdom, so on this evidence killing Cuo was never your intention. After the war the map was redrawn, kingdom exchanged for commandery, prince moved from one fief to another, and Liang was left standing until the king's death in 144, when it was split into five kingdoms for his five sons and his daughters were given revenues. What the peace bought is stated in the Shiji's treatise on the balanced standard, which Sima Guang reproduces in his closing survey of the reign: cash piled in the capital's treasury with the strings that held it rotting, and the old grain of the Great Granary overflowing outside and going bad. Sima Qian's own verdict on the cutting of the fiefs is that the lords had grown too strong and that Chao Cuo did not go about it by degrees — and that when the adviser Zhu Fu Yan spoke a generation later, the fiefs weakened and the realm was quiet. The hinge of safety and danger, he writes, is a matter of planning.
Shiji, juan 106; Shiji, juan 11 and 30; Hanshu, juan 5; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 16

In this episode

Six people carried 154 BCE: the minister whose plan outlived him, the commander who twice refused an order and was right both times, and the emperor who let them both be used.

Liu Pi劉濞
d. 154 BCE
King of Wu, sixty-two in 154 BCE, son of the founder's elder brother and therefore the founder's nephew. He had coins cast from his mountains and salt boiled from his sea, had not attended court for a generation, and lost a war that lasted three months.
Chao Cuo晁錯
d. 154 BCE
Imperial adviser turned Censor-in-chief, called the bag of wit of the eastern palace while he served the crown prince. He wrote the plan to cut the fiefs back and was delivered to the block in his court robes to buy the rebels' withdrawal.
Yuan Ang袁盎
d. 148 BCE
A former chancellor's rival and the one man the emperor had not let Chao Cuo destroy. He proposed the execution that was meant to end the war, carried the pardon to Wu, and was refused audience.
Zhou Yafu周亞夫
d. 143 BCE
Marquis of Jiang, commander-in-chief. The man whose camp the late emperor had been stopped outside for discipline. He let the rebels beat the empire's richest fief to pieces, cut their grain, and would not fight until they were starving.
Liu Wu劉武
d. 144 BCE
King of Liang, the emperor's full brother and the empire's richest prince. He held Suiyang through the siege, was deliberately abandoned while he held it, and spent the rest of his life getting even.
Emperor Jing劉啟
188–141 BCE
Liu Qi, second emperor of Han, the prince who had killed the heir of Wu with a gaming board. He approved the strategy that won, gave way to the one that did not, and said afterwards that he regretted it.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Killing Chao Cuo very nearly ended the revolt, and only Zhou Yafu's stubbornness threw away a chance at peace.

What the sources say

The histories say the offer was refused on the spot and the war ran on unchanged. The emperor's own annal records the pardon and the restoration of the land, and then adds that Yuan Ang was sent to announce it and that it did not stop them. The king of Wu would not kneel to receive the edict, and had already declared himself Emperor of the East. Two men in the record say the killing achieved nothing at all: an officer returned from the front, and a Marquis of Gongguo to a king who had surrendered.

Shiji, juan 11 and 101; Hanshu, juan 5 and 49
The story you may know

The war of the Seven Kingdoms was a long and exhausting struggle that nearly brought the dynasty down.

What the sources say

It lasted a season. The rising is dated to a specific day, the jiazi day of the first month, and the Shiji's summary is that the armies attacked and held each other for three months and Wu and Chu were broken; the emperor's annal puts the pursuit of the king of Wu at Dantu in the same spring and the deaths of the six other kings before the summer. Zhao alone held out for ten months. The exhaustion in the record is not the war's but the settlement's: the empire's wealth grows in the decades after it.

Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 5; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 16
The story you may know

The whole eastern half of the empire rose against Chang'an.

What the sources say

The coalition was seven kingdoms out of the many in the east, and two more never moved: the king of Qi regretted the agreement and poisoned himself, and the king of Jibei was held under guard by his own senior secretary and could not raise troops. Wu's own letter invited the kings of Huainan, Hengshan and Lujiang and an heir of old Changsha; none of them came. The Xiongnu, whom Zhao went to for help, sent nothing. The emperor's annal names the seven exactly, and they are the seven.

Shiji, juan 106; Hanshu, juan 5, Annals of Emperor Jing
The story you may know

The general who saved the dynasty died honoured, and the emperor he had served never forgot what he had done.

What the sources say

He was dismissed as chancellor, summoned to a banquet where a block of meat was set in front of him with no knife and no chopsticks, and told as he left that such a sulky man would not serve a young master. He was arrested over five hundred sets of store-made armour and shields his son had bought for his tomb and had gone unpaid on; asked why he was arming himself in the world below, he stopped answering, went hungry for five days and died vomiting blood. His king-of-Liang enemy had spent years telling the empress dowager against him, and had men kill the minister who had once been made chancellor in his place.

Shiji, juan 57; Hanshu, juan 40; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 16
The story you may know

Zhou Yafu's end was fate: the physiognomist Xu Fu had read a line in his face that ran into his mouth and told him he would starve in nine years.

What the sources say

Both official histories carry that prediction, and Sima Qian closes the biography with the words Sure enough, the Marquis of Jiang starved to death — a life arranged backwards around an outcome. The same chapters say the plain facts: he was picked out of Zhou Bo's sons as the ablest and given the marquisate, and he died in the hands of jailers who wanted a confession he would not give. Nothing in the accounts of 154 BCE requires the diviner, and the campaign's decisions were his own.

Shiji, juan 57; Hanshu, juan 40
