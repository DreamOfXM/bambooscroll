The Gentle Emperor

Luoyang, 25–57 CE — A farmer's younger brother, laughed at by his own family for caring about his fields, took the throne at thirty-one and spent three decades un-making the empire on purpose: he merged four hundred counties, sent his generals home rich, refused to march west, and left a restored dynasty whose emperors would never again be able to spend it.

01 / 18 Note
This episode is told from the Book of the Later Han: the two halves of the emperor's own annal, then the memoirs of the men and women in it — Deng Yu, Feng Yi and Cen Peng, Wu Han, the officers of the Hebei flight, Song Hong, Huan Tan, Zheng Xing, the empresses, the Western Regions. The ritual decisions and the census come from the treatises, which are Sima Biao's continuation of the dynasty's history, bound into this book; the court history of the day, the Eastern Wing's records, is used only where the commentaries quote it. The Zizhi Tongjian is a relay of these pages and is never a second witness. The later popular novels of the restoration appear only in the myth checks, where they are the thing being checked.
On our sources
02 / 18 In the sources
His elder brother Bo Sheng kept company with brave men and fed retainers, and the annal says he kept mocking Liu Xiu for worrying about farm work, comparing him to Gaozu xiong Zhong — the elder brother of the first emperor of Han, a nobody in the family story. He went to the capital in the Tianfeng years to take the Book of Documents, and when Nanyang starved he was selling grain at Wan, which is where the episode's politics start. One detail belongs to the machinery he later ran: years before the rising he rode to Yan You, a Wang Mang general, to plead a rent-arrears case for the marquis of his own village.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper, Annals of Emperor Guangwu
03 / 18 In the sources
The record of his start is a record of persuasion, not of enthusiasm: the annal says he at first did not accept it, and only weighed that his brother would certainly raise something great and that Wang Mang's ruin was already visible. The young men of the clan hid when they heard the rising had begun, saying Bo Sheng is going to get us killed; then they saw Liu Xiu in the crimson robe and the tall cap of an officer and were startled into settling down — even the careful one is doing it. He began on an ox, and got a horse only by killing the commandant of Xinye. When the loot was shared out unevenly and the crowd turned on the Liu family, he collected what his own kinsmen had taken and gave that away too.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper
04 / 18 In the sources
The storm broke the army, and the book's own figure for the dead is tens of thousands driven into the Zhui river until the water would not run; the survivors crossed over the corpses. The book says no dispatch of troops since Qin had been so grand, and a falling star and a collapsing hill of cloud fell on the camp first. Yan You's admiration is the annal's, and it is worth noting what he had heard: that this young man took no goods, only met soldiers and made plans. The officers had said the same about him in plainer terms before the battle — that he was timid in front of small enemies, so this was strange.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper; juan 1 lower, the closing hymn
05 / 18 In the sources
The annal keeps all four of those facts in one sentence, which is the closest thing in the book to a portrait of how he thought: never to boast of the rank of Kunyang, never to mourn Bo Sheng, food and laughter as usual. In 39 CE he had the brothers named after death — Bo Sheng as the martial duke of Qi, the other elder brother as the pitiful duke of Lu — and in the spring of 41 CE he built a shrine for four generations of the Chongling line while his own clan women drank in the old house. He also, in the ninth month of 25 CE, issued an edict making it a capital crime to harm the deposed emperor Liu Xuan, whom he had only just enfeoffed as the king of Huaiyang.

06 / 18 In the sources
The annal's sentence after the weeping is the point: from then on the discerning attached their hearts to him. On the road north in the tenth month of 23 CE he carried the staff of office, heard cases from the second-rank governors down to the subordinate clerks, released prisoners, abolished Wang Mang's pressing ordinances and restored the old names of the Han offices; the people came out with oxen and wine. Note what is not in this paragraph: no mass rising, no army, no claim to the throne. He was a subordinate of another emperor's government, and the restoration he is remembered for began as a change of uniform and paperwork.

07 / 18 In the sources
The annal does not spare this year: he slept by the road, his face split open by frost, and at the Hutuo river there were no boats and the ice took him only until a few carts went through and sank. Feng Yi's memoir adds the meals — bean porridge at the Wu Lou post-house in the cold, then wheat and turnip greens by a roofless shelter in the wind and rain — and says that twenty years later the emperor was still reciting the debt, that the porridge of the urgent days at Wulou and the wheat at the Hutuo had gone long unrepaid. The men who saved him were the minor officers of Xinhe and Shanggu and Yuyang, and the cavalry that arrived from the two northern commanderies under Wu Han and Kou Xun.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper; juan 17, Biography of Feng Yi; juan 21
08 / 18 In the sources
His word for it is in the annal: let the uneasy ones set themselves at ease. The same year the record gives the other half of his method. He would not fight the Bronze Horse bands when they challenged, cut their grain instead, and after he had surrounded and taken them he let each chief keep his own command, then rode alone through the ranks of men who had not yet been disarmed. They said of it that the Prince of Xiao puts his red heart into a man's belly — how then not to die for it — and he ended with several hundred thousand men, and the west of the pass called him the Bronze Horse emperor. In the same year he was very nearly killed at Shunshui on horseback, and the army believed him dead for days.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper
09 / 18 In the sources
The prayer is preserved in full, and it is an argument, not a eulogy: he repeats what his ministers asserted, that Wang Mang seized the throne and he rose in anger, that he broke Wang Xun and Wang Yi at Kunyang and killed Wang Lang and absorbed the Bronze Horse bands, and then it quotes a chart-text, Liu Xiu raises troops to arrest the unprincipled, Mao Jin cultivates virtue and becomes Son of Heaven — Mao Jin being the house name Liu written out in its parts. He refuses three times, as the protocol required, and accepts. The year-name became Jianwu, the first martial, and the town of Hao was renamed Gaoyi, the high town. The same month the Red Eyebrows set up their own puppet; the empire he describes as pacified was, on the day he said it, four-way civil war.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper; juan 97, Treatise on Sacrifices, upper
10 / 18 In the sources
Everything the annal says about the choice is a date and a hall. It does not rename the city and it does not call it the eastern capital — that usage is later. The circumstances were the ones he did not advertise: the old capital's palaces had been burned and its graves dug up by the Red Eyebrows, the eleven former emperors' spirit tablets had to be carried east by Deng Yu's staff, and the granary road he now needed ran along the Yellow River valley, where he already held the offices. His ministers in the same month were a twenty-four-year-old as Grand Minister over the Masses with a fief of ten thousand households, a horse-dealer as Marshal-in-Chief, and a district official of Yewang as Grand Minister of Works; the old man made Senior Grand Tutor had once been a county clerk.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper; juan 16, Biography of Deng Yu
11 / 18 Debated
He answered the academician with the sentence that is the whole settlement: of old, states were lost through a lack of principle, and no one ever heard of a state perishing because its meritorious officials had too much land. The seals were handed over the same day, and the investiture document told the marquises to be haughty without danger and full without overflowing, and to hand the fief down as a barrier for Han. Read against the rest of the reign, though, the bargain was never felt as fair by anyone: Huan Tan's memorial says the rewards and grants were thin and that the empire was not settled in time because of it; the governor of Yuyang rebelled in 26 CE on the ground that he and his colleague had done equal service and been paid equally little; and Ma Yuan, given a whole large county for the south, wrote that Lu Bode had opened seven commanderies and been given only a few hundred households, and asked how so little merit could carry so thick a reward for long.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper; juan 28 upper, Biography of Huan Tan; juan 12; juan 24, Biography of Ma Yuan
12 / 18 In the sources
The list is a policy, and it is in the annal year by year: the demobilisation edict of 31 CE sent the light chariots, the horse, the material officers and the naval men back to the ranks of the common people; the land tax in 30 CE was put back at one part in thirty, which had been the settled Western Han rate and not the wartime tenth; in 37 CE four kings of the blood were demoted to marquises because their rank did not accord with the classics, and with them the whole roll of one hundred and thirty-odd prince's sons and heirless marquises, while three more kings were stepped down to dukes; in 42 CE the provincial governors were struck and the inspectors put back; in 50 CE the emoluments of every post of a thousand shi and above were cut below the Western Han rates, and those below them raised. The treatise of commanderies and kingdoms says of its own list that every county named in the earlier book and missing here was merged away by this emperor.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 lower; juan 109, Treatise on Commanderies and Kingdoms
13 / 18 In the sources
He named his model himself, and it was not a founder but Emperor Wen, whose bare grave at Baling was the one that survived the fall of the Western Han intact — the practical reason for a small tomb is the army that digs up the large ones. His dying order of 57 CE is the same thought turned into procedure: he had done the people no good, so let everything follow Emperor Wen's制度 and be reduced and sparing; the inspectors and the second-rank governors were not to leave their cities, and no clerk was to be sent, and no memorial to come up the postal relay. The state funeral of the man who restored the dynasty was to be a piece of correspondence nobody had to answer.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 lower
14 / 18 In the sources
The phrase is his own, ruo dao, the soft way, and the annal sets it beside the family shrine he then built for every branch of the Chongling Liu. What the record actually documents, though, is legislation rather than temper. In 26 CE people who had sold a wife or a child to keep alive were declared free to go home to their parents and anyone who held them was to be prosecuted; in 28 CE no clerk under six hundred shi could be imprisoned without a prior report, and no man of eighty or boy of ten or woman implicated by her husband could be put in chains; in 35 CE the edict said that among the natures of heaven and earth man is the precious thing, and that those who killed their slaves and bond-servants should not have their sentences cut for it; in 31 CE he ordered that memorials to him must not call him sage. In the same spring of 41 CE he deposed Empress Guo to be the queen dowager of Zhongshan and made the lady Yin empress, and his historian noticed that this, too, was handled so that later ages could see no gap between the thick and the thin of his treatment.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper and lower; juan 10 upper, Annals of the Empresses
15 / 18 In the sources
The charge against them in the annal is four characters: the audit was not done truthfully. Then the land answered. The great families of the commanderies, the men who held the local soldiers, and the ordinary bandits rose everywhere in the same months, hurting and killing the chiefs of offices, and when the authorities came they scattered and when the authorities left they massed again; the annal names the four provinces of the east and north as the worst. The court did not send an army. It sent a proclamation: five bandits who cut down one of their number went free; officers who had hung back or let them go deliberately were not to be charged; a local chief who simply failed to catch them was not blamed, and only those who hid the fact were punished. The chiefs were then transplanted to another commandery, given fields and a ration, and told to settle down to live.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 lower
16 / 18 In the sources
The annal's reason for refusing is six characters: the middle regions had but newly settled, and there was no leisure for affairs outside them. The Western Regions memoir tells the same refusal with the numbers wrong — eighteen kings instead of sixteen — and gives the sequel: the king of Shache went on attacking, killed and carried off over a thousand people at Shanshan, and when the answer came back as above, Shanshan and the Tangut lands of the Turfan oasis went over to the Xiongnu again. The memoir ends the account with its own arithmetic: the Western Regions were severed from us for sixty-five years and then reopened. The reopening is the next life but one, and the man who did it was a copy-clerk with thirty-six companions.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 lower; juan 88, Memoir of the Western Regions
17 / 18 In the sources
The text was the chart of the meeting at Mount Tai, which promised the fortune of the red Liu's ninth generation on that mountain, and the annal says he was moved by it: he ordered his officers to go back through the river and Luo charts for anything about a ninth-generation封禅, they reported findings, and he agreed. In the second month of 56 CE he burned the sacrifice facing Tai Shan, climbed and sealed it, and made the rite at Liangfu below; the stone inscription cut that spring is preserved and it quotes the chart lines, including the one that had brought him the throne. The same year he built the Bright Hall, the Spirit Terrace and the Round College, and — the annal's sentence, of 56 CE — he promulgated the charts and the apocrypha to the whole realm. When he asked Huan Tan where the Spirit Terrace should go and said he meant to decide it by the apocrypha, Tan said after a long silence that he did not read them, then went on at length that they were not classics; the emperor called that slandering the sages and having no law and was about to have him beheaded, and Tan's forehead bled on the tiles before he was let go, and he died of illness on the road to his post in Lu'an. Zheng Xing, asked the same question about the suburban sacrifice, answered I do not read them and was forgiven; the memoir adds the consequence: he spoke often on public affairs and always out of the classics, and because he was not good at the apocrypha he was never given the highest employments. Chen Yuan said the same thing about a book: the Zuo tradition was taken up by his own judgment, and dropped halfway because its masters did not understand the charts.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 lower; juan 97, Treatise on Sacrifices, upper; juan 28 upper, Biography of Huan Tan; juan 36
18 / 18 Commentary
The annal's closing judgment is the settlement itself in five words and five more: the meritorious officials put back, the document officers brought forward, the bows and arrows put away and the horses and oxen turned out — the war that stops war. His son asked him once about formations and got the answer that a duke of Wei once asked Confucius the same and did not get it, and that this was not his son's concern; and asked not to work from dawn until the sun went west every single day on his health, he said he liked it and did not find it tiring. Two records of what that left. The treatise of commanderies and kingdoms gives the count of 57 CE: four million two hundred seventy-one thousand six hundred and thirty-four households, and twenty-one million seven thousand eight hundred and twenty mouths, with its own verdict on the hundred years of Wang Mang and the wars that the people were wasted and of ten only two remained; one printed form of that figure has three where the standard collation reads two. And Zhong Changtong, writing two generations after the collapse: the emperor grieved at the loss of power by several reigns and hated the strong minister's theft of the mandate, so he corrected the crooked past the straight, put no trust in what was below him, and though he appointed the three ministers, the business went to the terrace offices; the ministers were only there to be blamed. Power then moved to the families of empresses and to the attendants near the person — and of the first of those families, the ten-thousand-household marquis of 24 CE had been the ancestor of the men who sat in the palace and chose an emperor in 106 CE.
Hou Han shu, juan 1 lower; juan 109, Treatise on Commanderies and Kingdoms; juan 49, Biography of Zhong Changtong; juan 16

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Guangwu won his empire as a battlefield genius, the sword-throwing emperor of the popular retellings, who personally put a million men to flight at Kunyang.

What the sources say

Kunyang is in the record and so is his personal courage, but the record's own evidence for what he was good at lies elsewhere: he cut the Bronze Horse bands' grain for a month before he fought them, he wrote to his generals telling them to sit still, and when Wu Han disobeyed him at Chengdu in 36 CE he read him out in the same letter style — but I told you a thousand things and here you go throwing yourself into a battle. His ministers say the effect in one line: he consulted Deng Yu about every general he employed, and Deng Yu's picks all fitted the post, so the emperor was known for knowing men. The million and the forty-two thousand are the court's own numbers, repeated in the emperor's accession prayer and in the annal's closing hymn.

Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper and lower; juan 16, Biography of Deng Yu; juan 18, Biography of Wu Han
The story you may know

The restoration of the Han was a rising of the people which swept a reluctant landholder onto the throne in 25 CE.

What the sources say

Every step of his ascent in the record is the work of a few men with a stake in the name. A numerologist from Wan brought him a prophecy line to sell him on it; his own kinsmen hid from the rising; a puppet emperor's government made him an inspector, and the annal's most quoted line about that job is an old clerk weeping to see the Han offices' dignity again. He was made emperor by a classmate riding in with a chart-text, a colonel warning him that the crowd would otherwise go home, and a memorial that compared the omen to the white fish of Zhou. The portents of his birth — the red light in the room, the single stalk of grain with nine ears that gave him his name — are collected in the annal by his historian, who ends the list with a question rather than a claim.

Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper; juan 1 lower, the historian's essay
The story you may know

His mildness proves the Eastern Han state was soft: a government that could not even count its own land.

What the sources say

The land-and-household audit of 39 CE shows a state sharp enough to kill its own top officials over a return: the governor of the capital commandery and ten-odd governors died in prison in 40 CE, and two Grand Ministers over the Masses died in the same business. What happened next is the real story. When the great families and the men who commanded the local soldiers rose in four provinces at once, the court did not send an army after them; it forgave the officers who had let them through, promised a pardon to any five bandits who killed one of their number, moved the chiefs to another commandery and gave them fields. The state stopped being exact about land and stayed exact about everything else, and the annal's own line about cattle grazing with the town gates open is how it congratulated itself on the compromise.

Hou Han shu, juan 1 lower
The story you may know

The prophetic charts and apocrypha were a fringe superstition hovering at the edges of his court, a private oddity of an ageing emperor.

What the sources say

They were the court's constitutional material, and the record dates them precisely. The text that made him emperor was a chart-line, and he read it aloud to Heaven on the altar at Hao in 25 CE; in 56 CE he promulgated the charts and the apocrypha to the whole realm in the same year as he built the Bright Hall and the Spirit Terrace. The Feng and Shan he had refused with a threat in 54 CE he performed in 56 CE because he had read a chart one night during his fast and had his officers go and find more. Two scholars who said flatly that they did not read them nearly died of it and spent the rest of their careers out of the great offices; a whole school tradition of the Zuo commentary was stopped in mid-flight, in a minister's words, because its masters did not understand the charts.

Hou Han shu, juan 1 upper and lower; juan 97, Treatise on Sacrifices; juan 28 upper; juan 36
The story you may know

The Eastern Han was the Western Han carried on under the same name, restored by a descendant to the same machinery.

What the sources say

The name was restored; the state was rebuilt smaller and differently run. More than four hundred counties were folded into others in 30 CE with one officer in ten kept; the commandery commandants who drilled the annual levy were abolished in the same year; the light chariots, the horse and the naval levies were stood down in 31 CE and sent back to civilian registration; the land tax went to one in thirty; the provincial governors became inspectors again in 42 CE; four kings of the blood and a hundred and thirty-odd other princes were stepped down in 37 CE; and in 50 CE the pay scale above a thousand shi was cut below the old Western Han rates. His own register counted four million households and some twenty-one million mouths at his death in 57 CE, against the treatise's memory of the fullest Western Han count — of the people, its sentence says, ten parts left two. Zhong Changtong's verdict on the machinery, written late in the dynasty: the three ministers were appointed, and the business went to the terrace offices.
