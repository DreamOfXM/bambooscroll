The Deep Roots

The Partisan Prohibitions, 166–184 CE — The throne's servants called the empire's best-known men a faction and outlawed them; two prohibitions, eighteen years apart, turned the honour-roll of the age into a criminal register — and the gentry learned from it that the court was something to be survived, not served.

03 / 18 Debated
The two-gates idea — the official world's recommendation system as a rival gate of admission to the one the imperial in-laws and eunuchs controlled — is a modern historians' reading of the period — the classic statement of it is Chen Yinke's account of the scholar clans and the throne's servants. The sources show it working: officials and students trading and building one another's names, and the palace's men calling the traffic a fang.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions; modern scholarship
04 / 18 In the sources
Cheng Jin and Cen Ye stand in the memoir, with the governor who did the same in the north: officials killing the palace's protected men, and the court answering with prison — Cheng Jin's case was settled before he could be tried, and the others died in the capital's jail. The accusation that bound such deaths together, that the officials kept a private court of reputation against the throne, came from the eunuchs; the emperor's word for it was fang.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions

The first round — more than two hundred taken — is in the Partisan Prohibitions memoir. The taxonomy of ranks is preserved from Sima Biao's history of the dynasty; the rolls vary slightly between its copies, but the age agreed on the shape, and the memoir itself treats the naming as the real offence.

The biography of the eunuchs states the grievance in their own terms — the officers refused to bend the law to their kin and guests, and so made an enemy of every one of them. Li Ying's own famous sentence from the jail, in which he accepted death and regretted only that the Han's fall would not wait on him, is given by the memoir of his house; the wording as we quote it is compressed, as its wording there is.

The empress-dowager's plea and her leaving the hall are in her memoir and the Partisan Prohibitions memoir; the release came in 167 with the new reign-title, and with it the lifelong prohibition from office — the gu of dang-gu, the bolt. Fan Pang's lines are his recorded speech at the second prohibition, when he came to the executions to salute the dying.

08 / 18 In the sources
The acclamation of Ling emperor and the regency are in the annals and in Dou Wu's and Chen Fan's biographies; the recorded reason for the choice — simplicity and youth — is there too, and so is its sequel: the plan to destroy the eunuchs was delayed, and the eunuchs were not waiting.

09 / 18 In the sources
The coup of the eighth month of 168 stands in Chen Fan's and Dou Wu's biographies and the Tongjian: the oath to the emperor, the bribed gates, Dou Wu's kin and guests coming back with the guard, the regent's death outside the gate, Chen Fan's death with his cap-strings undone. His own last recorded line is the rule of the whole affair — he would not serve an emperor by a letter of resignation.
Hou Han shu, Hereditary Biographies of Chen Fan and Dou Wu; Zizhi Tongjian, the Later Han chronicles
10 / 18 In the sources
The settlement of 168 — the market exposure, the exile of the houses, the proscription of the retainers — stands in the two biographies. That the eunuchs used the settling to dispatch private enmities is stated in the memoir of them as well as the partisans: the case grew sideways by the simple arithmetic of accusation.
Hou Han shu, Hereditary Biographies of Chen Fan and Dou Wu; Memoir of the Eunuchs
11 / 18 In the sources
The second prohibition is the memoir's own narrative: the forged case, Li Ying and Du Mi first, the emperor's question and the answer that turned association itself into the crime. The toll the memoir records is six or seven hundred dead, transported, attainted and disqualified, and the Hou Han shu's epitaph on the year says it plainly: the gentlemen of the empire were cut off, and with them the last hold of the dynasty. Fan Pang's words above are his farewell to his son as he was led out.

The extension of the charges to pupils, friends and their houses stands in the memoir. Sima Biao's history preserves the rank-by-rank taxonomy, though who filled each place varies between its copies, and the memoir itself dates the naming to the party's own talk rather than to any register. The totals of the proscribed differ between sources.

13 / 18 In the sources
The flight of Zhang Jian is told as a hunt across whole commands, and the four-character phrase the Hou Han shu spends on it became an idiom. The memoir sets beside it the pursuers' counter-measure — rewards of a thousand in gold, and the same crime charged to whoever sheltered him — and states that the houses which took him in risked their clan's extinction and took him in anyway.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions
14 / 18 In the sources
Du Mi's self-arrest is in the memoir, where his name stands beside Li Ying's at the head of the second list. The Hou Han shu gives the moral arithmetic of the age in one paired stroke: the fugitive carried from house to house at the risk of whole clans, and the man at his desk refusing to put anyone to that risk.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions
15 / 18 In the sources
The Kong house of Queshan is in the memoir of Kong Rong as well as in the Partisan Prohibitions memoir: the shelter, the three claims of guilt, and the court's choice of the elder brother. The rule that made a night's lodging a capital charge — the pursuers' rewards and the equal guilt of harbourers — is the same memoir's.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions
16 / 18 In the sources
The degrees of the prohibition, the three and the five, are the memoir's; so is the emperor's own line that men who harboured the party's feelings were the party. The Hou Han shu draws the consequence in one sentence the whole episode is written to prove: from this point the gentlemen were parted from the court, and the court from everything else.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions
17 / 18 In the sources
The lifting of the prohibitions under the Yellow Turban blow stands in the Partisan Prohibitions memoir: the mid-year's amnesty, and the warning attributed to the Regular Attendant Lü Qiang that a long-banished gentry would take the rebels' side in a moment. The Hou Han shu is candid about the cause: the outlaws were needed as soldiers, and the empire's danger outran its grudge. The families under the bolt were sent back to their commanderies.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions; Annals of Emperor Ling
18 / 18 Note
The Hou Han shu's judgment is that the prohibitions broke the empire's bones and its sinews together. The larger reading — that the bans turned the gentry against the court as a class and made the great families' politics of the next four centuries — belongs to modern historians (the two-gates thesis and the debates after it), and paraphrases them, not any Han source.
Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions; modern scholarship

In this episode

Seven people carried 166 to 184 CE: the critics who named each other as worth dying for, the regent who tried to use them, the eunuch who outlawed them, and the emperor who was too young to know which side he had chosen.

d. 169 CE
The judge whose severity made the eunuchs' kin afraid to leave their gates. The first name on the faction's lists, and the first great name it killed.
Zhang Jian張儉

The governor who would not run: the order to arrest him reached his own commandery while he sat at his office, and he went to the prison-wagon of his own will.
Fan Pang范滂

Chen Fan陳蕃

Grand Tutor, eighty-ish and unbought; in 168 he tried to wipe the inner court out in one morning and died with his cap-strings undone.
Dou Wu竇武

Regent and general-in-chief for one season, empress-dowager's kinsman; betrayed by the officers he trusted, he died outside the capital rather than be taken.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

The Partisan Prohibitions were one purge: the emperor struck the scholars in 166 and the matter was settled.

What the sources say

There were two prohibitions eighteen years apart, under two emperors — the first bolted the released prisoners to civil death in 167, the second destroyed them in 169 and extended the outlawry to their schools and families — and the end came only in 184, forced by the Yellow Turban emergency, not by any change of mind at the centre.

Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions
The story you may know

The party was a real organisation with a register, office-bearers and membership oaths, as the court's charge alleged.

What the sources say

The lists were the public culture of reputation itself — Three Lords and Eight Elites were names the age's talk hung on men, which Sima Biao then copied — and the only register the record shows is the palace's own roll of the proscribed. That the accused had no organisation is why the fugitive Zhang Jian could be hunted across a year of provinces without a single captured letter of instructions.

Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions; Sima Biao, Xu hanshu
The story you may know

Li Ying and the partisans were meek, gentle scholars done in by malice.

What the sources say

They were governors who ruled by the letter and the prison: Li Ying's execution of a man who had read an amnesty in advance and drilled his son to kill inside its cover — the pair were caught, pardoned, and then put to death anyway by the governor who could not stomach it — was lawful by the letter of Han statute on the great crime; what it was not was diplomatic, and the accusation that opened the prohibitions came out of that household. The sources mourn the party's fall as the empire's loss; they do not pretend its members were harmless men.

Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions
The story you may know

The partisans were killed in prison by the tens of thousands during the prohibitions.

What the sources say

The Hou Han shu's own figures are smaller and more precise: two hundred and odd named men implicated in the first round, six or seven hundred dead, transported, attainted and disqualified in the second, with the ban on office carried out to the five degrees of kinship. The age's horror came from who died — the first families of the empire, legally, in the emperor's own name — not from the count.

Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions
The story you may know

Emperor Ling lifted the prohibitions when he came to see the partisans had been right.

What the sources say

The lifting of 184 was a war measure. The outlawed military families were needed against a rising that took the eastern provinces at once, and the argument was made to the emperor as policy — by the Regular Attendant Lü Qiang, a eunuch, who warned that men long under the bolt would join the rebels without a second thought. The emperor agreed, and the dynasty died anyway: it simply died with the gentry's help rather than in their teeth.

Hou Han shu, juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions; Annals of Emperor Ling
