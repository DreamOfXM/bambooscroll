Seven Days at Baideng

Pingcheng, 200 BCE — The founder of the dynasty crossed the frontier passes ahead of his own infantry, was held on a tableland for seven days by an enemy his defectors had informed, and bought the next sixty years with a borrowed princess and an annual cargo of silk and grain.

01 / 18 Note
This line is told only from the official histories. For 200 BCE that means the Records of the Grand Historian — its memoir of the Xiongnu, its memoirs of Han Xin and Lu Wan, of Liu Jing, of Chen Ping — and the Book of Han's annals of Emperor Gaozu and its own Xiongnu memoir, with the Zizhi Tongjian where it gathers those books into one year. Nothing from a novel, an opera, a film or a game enters a panel. Where those traditions add something, the Myth Check at the end says so and names its own source.
On our sources
02 / 18 In the sources
The Annals of the Xiongnu tell it flatly. Touman meant to disinherit the prince, so he handed him to the Yuezhi as a hostage and then attacked them; Modu stole a good horse and rode out of it, was thought remarkable, and was given ten thousand riders. He made the whistling arrow, killed everyone who hesitated at it, killed his father, and then his stepmother, his brother and every official who would not take the order. The same chapter has the Eastern Hu ask him for a thousand-li horse, then for a wife, then for a strip of grazing nobody lived on — and Modu, having given the horse and the woman, execute every councillor who said the grass could be spared: land is the root of a state.
Shiji, juan 110, Annals of the Xiongnu
03 / 18 In the sources
Han Xin was an illegitimate grandson of King Xiang of the old royal house of Han and stood, the record says, eight chi five cun tall; he had been made king of Han, then moved to Taiyuan in 201 because the land he held lay against the empire's toughest recruiting districts — near Gong and Luoyang, hemmed in by Wan and Ye, with Huaiyang on its east. Besieged at Mayi he sent intermediaries to the Xiongnu more than once; the Han court read that as two hearts and blamed him. Fearing execution he made a pact with Modu, gave Mayi up and fell on Taiyuan. The same book keeps a letter he wrote years later, from the other side, counting the three things he could not be forgiven for.
Shiji, juan 93, Memoir of Han Xin and Lu Wan; Hanshu, juan 1, Annals of Emperor Gaozu
04 / 18 In the sources
Liu Jing's report and its consequence are in his own biography. The emperor, whose army had already crossed the Ju Zhu pass, called him a slave of Qi who had got his office with his tongue, said he was spreading defeat, and had him put in irons at Guangwu. The Annals of the Xiongnu record the other half of the same trick: Modu hid his strong cattle and put the weak and old forward to be seen.
Shiji, juan 99, Memoir of Liu Jing and Shusun Tong; Shiji, juan 110
05 / 18 In the sources
The campaign's opening is in the memoir of Han Xin: Tongdi, then Manqiu Chen and Wang Huang with the Xiongnu's Left and Right Wise Kings and over ten thousand horse south of Guangwu, then another victory at Lishi, then horse massing again northwest of Loufan and broken by Han chariot and cavalry. Then the Xiongnu chapter adds the scale: the emperor mobilised the whole army — it gives three hundred and twenty thousand, heavy with infantry — and went north after the feigned retreats. The frostbite figure is the historian's claim, and so is every number of men in this episode.
Shiji, juan 93; Shiji, juan 110; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 11
06 / 18 In the sources
One sentence in the Xiongnu memoir carries the whole tactical fact: the emperor arrived at Pingcheng first, and the foot had not all come up. Modu then let his picked cavalry loose. The same passage fixes what closed around the tableland in a piece of detail nobody would invent: on the west face all the horses were white, on the east all dappled, on the north all black, on the south all bay. A ring sorted by coat is a review, not a mob.
Shiji, juan 110; Hanshu, juan 94a, Memoir of the Xiongnu
07 / 18 Debated
The two histories do not agree on the size of the ring, and the site is not going to pick for them. The Shiji's Xiongnu memoir says Modu loosed four hundred thousand of picked cavalry; the Hanshu's, which copies the passage almost word for word, gives three hundred thousand and odd — and the Shiji's own earlier sentence, counting the men who drew bows across the whole confederation, gives the same three hundred thousand and odd. The emperor's annal in the Hanshu disposes of the whole affair in one clause: hemmed in by the Xiongnu, seven days, got out by Chen Ping's secret plan.
Shiji, juan 110; Hanshu, juan 94a; Hanshu, juan 1, Annals of Emperor Gaozu
08 / 18 In the sources
Her argument is preserved in both histories: two masters do not corner each other; even if you win the Han's land, in the end you cannot live on it; and besides, the Han king has something divine about him — look at that. Modu had also appointed a rendezvous with Wang Huang and Zhao Li, generals of the defected king of Han, and their troops did not arrive; the Shiji says he concluded they were treating with the Han, took his wife's reasons as well, and opened one corner of the encirclement.
Shiji, juan 110; Hanshu, juan 94a
09 / 18 In the sources
That instruction is in the memoir of Han Xin, where Chen Ping holds the post of Protector of the Army. He is made to say that the Hu are armed to a man, so let the strong crossbows be strung and two arrows set facing outwards, and let the column walk out slowly. The Xiongnu memoir tells the same walk in its own words — at the full draw, the arrows to the outside, straight out through the corner that was undone — and adds that the Xiongnu horse drew back by degrees, that a heavy fog came up, and that parties of Han went backwards and forwards through them unperceived until the emperor was inside Pingcheng and the relieving army had arrived.
Shiji, juan 93; Shiji, juan 110; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 11
10 / 18 In the sources
The apology is Liu Jing's own biography. It also keeps the insult thrown at him on the road north — slave of Qi, you got your office with your tongue — on the same page, without embarrassment. The reward for having been right was two thousand households, the rank of Marquis Within the Passes, and a title, Jianxin, Established Trust. The ten parties of assessors are dead on the emperor's own statement, inside the apology above; the book charges them with nothing.
Shiji, juan 99; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 11
11 / 18 In the sources
Liu Jing's statement of the case is preserved: the realm had just been settled and its soldiers were worn out, so the north could not be subdued by arms; and Modu had killed his own father and taken his father's wives and ruled by force, so he could not be moved by decency. Only a long calculation on the scale of grandchildren was left. Its mechanism was a marriage — make the Chanyu a son-in-law, then a grandfather — and Liu Jing warned in advance that it would fail if Han sent somebody else's girl and called her a princess, because the Hu would know it and she would not be honoured.
Shiji, juan 99; Shiji, juan 110
12 / 18 Debated
The Shiji says the emperor agreed, was refused at home, and in the end could not send the Eldest Princess: he took a girl of the ordinary palace households, gave her the name of the Eldest Princess, and married her to the Chanyu. The Hanshu's Xiongnu memoir is drier and more precise: a daughter of the imperial clan of the second rank — a king's daughter, not the emperor's — was sent to be the Chanyu's wife, since in Han usage only an emperor's daughter by the empress was a princess. Both versions grant the objection Liu Jing had raised.
Shiji, juan 99; Hanshu, juan 94a
13 / 18 In the sources
The terms are one sentence in the Xiongnu memoir: each year the court was to send the Xiongnu floss and silk cloth, wine, rice and food, each in a stated amount, the two sides were to swear brotherhood and seal it with a marriage, and Modu's pressure eased for a while. The Hanshu copies the sentence, drops the rice from it, and swaps the Shiji's elder-brother-and-younger-brother wording for the plain word for brothers; it is the same oath. The trade that hung off the pact is recorded under Jingdi a generation later in the same chapter: the markets opened, gifts sent to the Xiongnu, a princess dispatched, as by the old agreement.
Shiji, juan 110; Hanshu, juan 94a
14 / 18 In the sources
When a Han general wrote offering him pardon and his old rank, Han Xin answered with a letter the Shiji keeps in full: he had three crimes — he had not died at Xingyang, he had not held Mayi, and he now led the Hu's cavalry against Han; his people, he said, were like the men of Wu and Qin who had been used up, and coming back was not possible. They fought at Canhe, which the Han took and sacked, and he was killed there. Zhang Sheng, the man Lu Wan sent to the Xiongnu, is in the same book: talked into advising Yan to keep the frontier war burning, pretended-executed by his own court so that he could keep working, and found at the Xiongnu court when a deserter told the emperor.
Shiji, juan 93
15 / 18 In the sources
The Xiongnu memoir calls Modu's letter to Empress Lü improper, words out of place; it records her intention to march and keeps the generals' objection in one clause: if Gaozu's worth and his arms were not enough, and he was still caught at Pingcheng, what are we. She dropped the campaign and renewed the marriage. The defeat was then used as a fact of policy for a generation; it was still the first argument against a northern war when a frontier ambush failed in the 130s.
Shiji, juan 110
16 / 18 In the sources
Liu Jing came back from the Xiongnu with the distance that mattered: the kings of the Baiyang and Loufan in the Ordos were, at nearest, seven hundred li from Chang'an, and light horse could reach the Wei valley in a day and a night, while the valley — newly ravaged — was thinly peopled and good land. So he asked for the Tian of Qi, the Zhao, Qu and Jing of Chu, the descendants of Yan, Zhao, Han and Wei and the region's famous strongmen to be moved in: at peace, they would face the Hu; if the lords moved, there would be enough of them to march east. The Shiji names the policy: strengthen the root, weaken the branches. The emperor agreed, and Liu Jing moved the people.
Shiji, juan 99
17 / 18 Commentary
Chen Ping's biography says the emperor used Chen Ping's marvellous device, that the Chanyu's wife was reached with it, and that the ring was opened by it — and then that his devices were largely secret, so that no one in the age ever heard them; the Hanshu annal is content with one clause: he got out by Chen Ping's secret plan. What is not secret is the bribe carried through the lines, the Xiongnu rendezvous that the Han defectors failed to keep, and the march-order quoted above. Late in life, made right chancellor under the new emperor, Chen Ping is reported to have said of himself: I have taken too many dark counsels; that is what the teachers of the Yin and Yang forbid. If our house is degraded, that is the end of it — because of the many hidden wrongs I carry.
Shiji, juan 56, Memoirs of Chancellor Chen; Shiji, juan 93; Hanshu, juan 1
18 / 18 In the sources
Han Xin's line was raised in the north: he went over with his crown prince, a son was born at the town of Tuitang and was named for it, and the crown prince in turn had a son called Ying. In 166 BCE Tuitang and Ying brought their followers south and surrendered. They were made marquises of Gongguo and of Xiangcheng, and of the war of the Seven Kingdoms twelve years later the Shiji says one sentence about the first of them: the Marquis of Gongguo out-did all the generals. The marriage-tribute system ended in the 130s, when a merchant of Mayi offered to betray the town and the Chanyu, riding up to its walls, found them empty of men, took a watchman off a beacon and went home; the emperor who had planned the ambush drove the general who had refused the follow-up to suicide.
Shiji, juan 93; Shiji, juan 108, Memoir of Han Changru; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 18

In this episode

Six people carried 200 BCE. Two of them were arguing about whether the empire could pay for its own frontier, one was a woman the court never consulted, and the sixth was the steppe itself.

Liu Bang劉邦
256–195 BCE
Emperor Gaozu of Han, founder. Took the field in the tenth month against a vassal's revolt, reached Pingcheng before his own army, and spent seven days inside a closed ring of cavalry. On the way home he apologised to a man he had put in irons and told the army he had killed the ten who had advised the march.
Modu冒頓單于
d. 174 BCE
Chanyu of the Xiongnu. He drilled his escort with a whistling arrow, killed his father with it, took a horse, a wife and a thousand li of empty grazing from the Eastern Hu, then hid his fat cattle and strong riders to lure the Han north.
Han Xin韓信
d. 196 BCE
King of Han on the frontier, and not the marquis with the same name. Besieged at Mayi, scolded by his own court for treating for peace, he handed the town to the Xiongnu and gave the emperor the war he was caught in. He died leading Xiongnu cavalry five years later.
Chen Ping陳平
d. 178 BCE
The emperor's planter of stratagems, ennobled six times for counsels the record says were kept too secret to be heard. At Baideng what it will let him say is an order of march: crossbows strung, two arrows nocked to the outside, and walk.
Liu Jing劉敬
fl. 202–198 BCE
A conscript from Qi in a sheepskin coat who argued the court into camping the capital inside the pass, then reported that the Xiongnu could not be attacked and was chained for it. Released with a marquisate of two thousand households, he wrote the marriage-tribute agreement and proposed moving over a hundred thousand people into the Wei valley.
Empress Lu呂后
d. 180 BCE
Wife of the founder, mother of the heir and of one daughter, and the power in the court after 195 BCE. The bargain on the table needed that daughter. She wept day and night, and the girl who went north was somebody else.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

A Han bride for the Chanyu was chosen from a portrait, and lost her face — and her marriage — because she would not bribe the painter.

What the sources say

That story is in the Xijing zaji, a Jin-dynasty anecdote collection, where the painter Mao Yanshou and his colleagues are executed for it. It belongs to 33 BCE, not to this bargain, and the Hanshu's own sentence has no painter in it: the emperor gave a palace lady of good family, Wang Qiang, courtesy name Zhaojun, to the Chanyu who asked for a Han marriage. And in 200 BCE nobody of the emperor's blood went at all.

Xijing zaji (anecdote collection, tier D); against Hanshu, juan 94b, Memoir of the Xiongnu
The story you may know

Chen Ping broke the encirclement with a marvel — a painter's picture of Han beauty, sent to make the Chanyu's wife jealous.

What the sources say

The Shiji credits him with a marvellous device and then says the plan was kept so secret that the age never heard of it; the Hanshu's annal has the same thing as a secret plan. The jealous-beauty version is a guess made four centuries later by Huan Tan, in a lost essay collection quoted by commentators. What the records will actually show is a bribe carried through the lines, a rendezvous the Han defectors failed to keep, a fog, and a march-order: crossbows strung, two arrows nocked outward, walk.

Huan Tan, Xinlun (1st c. CE), lost and quoted in later commentary; against Shiji, juan 56, 93 and 110
The story you may know

The heqin marriage made the Xiongnu subordinate to Han, and the annual deliveries were tribute paid by a subject people.

What the sources say

The two courts were written as equals in the same documents. The pact is a sworn brotherhood, and the Xiongnu chancery answered in its own style: the great Chanyu of the Xiongnu, set up by Heaven, respectfully inquires after the Emperor's welfare. When the court debated whether to fight after the Xiongnu had destroyed the Yuezhi, its ministers conceded the same premise from the other side: the land is salt marsh, it cannot be lived on, and the marriage is the cheaper option.

Shiji, juan 110; Hanshu, juan 94a
The story you may know

An emperor's own daughter was sacrificed to the steppe to save the dynasty.

What the sources say

The record says the opposite, twice, and blames no one grandly. Liu Jing had warned that a substitute would not be honoured; the emperor agreed, the empress dowager wept, and the book's sentence is blunt — the emperor could not send the Eldest Princess. A girl of the palace households was named in her place; the Hanshu calls her a daughter of the imperial clan of the second rank. She is unnamed in every one of these books.

Shiji, juan 99; Hanshu, juan 94a
The story you may know

After Baideng the Han abandoned the north and hid behind its wall.

What the sources say

The settlements of the period are the opposite of a withdrawal. The same adviser who wrote the marriage pact had the old royal houses and the powerful families of the east moved into the Wei valley — the record gives over a hundred thousand people — on the stated ground that the Ordos peoples were seven hundred li from the capital; the frontier markets stayed open under the treaty; and a later memorial against dismantling the outposts quotes border elders saying that the Xiongnu, since losing the Yin mountains, had never passed them without weeping.

Shiji, juan 99; Shiji, juan 110; Hanshu, juan 94b
