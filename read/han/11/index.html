Thirty-Six Men

The Western Regions, 73–102 CE — A clerk of thirty-one took a broom out of a government office and thirty-six men across the desert; one night's fire made an oasis kneel, and for thirty years the road to Rome lay open to a single Han officer and the men who chose to stay.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Ban Chao's name for the desert, Taklamakan, has meant “go in and don't come out” since antiquity.

What the sources say

The etymology is a modern folk one, and the desert's name is not Chinese anyway; what the sources give is the “great sands” and a chain of city-states. The terror is not in the name: it is in the record of what it took to cross on.

Hou Han shu, Memoir on the Western Regions; on the folk etymology, nothing earlier than modern travel writing
The story you may know

Ban Chao declared that he would rather love one fox or rabbit of his own court than the gold and jade of foreign lands.

What the sources say

A later apophthegm put in his mouth: the polished maxim appears in the telling, not in Ban Chao's Hou Han shu biography. What the record carries is his practice — fifty-odd states held with silk, hostages and titles, treasures sent in by submissive states, and no plunder attributed to him; the sentiment is Ban Chao's, the sentence is not.

Hou Han shu, juan 47, Hereditary Biographies of Ban Chao and Ban Liang; the maxim's wording is absent from the text
The story you may know

Gan Ying reached the Roman empire and stood in a provincial city of Syria.

What the sources say

The record says he came to the shore of the Western Sea and turned back without crossing. Most modern readings place the shore on the Persian Gulf, with the Parthians' scare-story about the sailing time deliberately told to keep the silk trade to themselves; taking him to Antioch asks the text to say more than it does.

Hou Han shu, Memoir on the Western Regions
The story you may know

Ban Chao won the west with armies: his thirty-six men grew into a garrison force, and the empire held the Silk Road on Chinese arms.

What the sources say

The arithmetic is what the story forgets. The western dominion ran on staffs of dozens and, at need, borrowed cavalry: at the end his army was Kucha's and Shanshan's levies with Kangju horse on loan. No pitched battle won by an army sent from the east decided any of it — the west was held by diplomacy with a knife behind it, by a garrison the size of a village.

Hou Han shu, juan 47, Hereditary Biographies of Ban Chao and Ban Liang
The story you may know

The Taklamakan road was a peaceful caravan route with an empire at its far end.

What the sources say

What the records give is a corridor that had to be won again every generation, with Han itself abandoning it for thirty years (76–94) and finally for good in 107; the thirty-six states counted by Han were thirty-six because they kept splitting, merging, and changing masters — a taxonomic running count, not a settled map.

Hou Han shu, Memoir on the Western Regions; juan 47, Hereditary Biographies of Ban Chao and Ban Liang
