Three Articles of Law

Into the Pass, 206 BCE — A rebel of fifty from a village in the east walked into the richest capital in the world, refused the palace, refused the treasury, refused the surrender king — and wrote the terms of an empire that would take his name for its own.
18 panels~9 min read free to readevery line sourced
01 / 18 Note
This episode is told from two books only: the Records of the Grand Historian, completed by Sima Qian around 91 BCE, and the Book of the Han, written a century later by Ban Gu and his family. Sima Qian had the court archives and the oral reports of men who had served under these commanders; where Ban Gu rewrites him, the note says so. Where the two are silent, this page is silent too.
On our sources
02 / 18 In the sources
The surrender is in the annals of Gaozu: plain coach, white horse, a cord about the neck, the imperial seals and commander's tallies sealed and given up, at the side of Zhi Road. The annals of the First Emperor tell the same surrender and add the number of days; Ban Gu's account of the entry follows Sima Qian closely and drops the number.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Emperor Gaozu; Basic Annals of the First Emperor of Qin; Hanshu, Annals of Emperor Gaozu
03 / 18 In the sources
“The King of Chu appointed me because I could tolerate; and besides, he has surrendered — to kill the surrendered is unlucky.” The annals give the reason in his own words, and then the flat sentence that he gave the King of Qin over to the officers. The killing came later, and it was not Liu Bang who did it: Xiang Yu's army marched into the capital and put Ziying to death.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Emperor Gaozu; Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
04 / 18 In the sources
The destruction of Zhao Gao is told in the biography of Li Si: the new king pleaded illness to draw him out, a eunuch named Han Tan struck him down in the ablutions palace, and three kin-groups were exterminated and paraded in the capital. The same biography carries what went before — the altered edict of 209 BCE that destroyed the rightful heir, the deer presented to the court as a horse, the second emperor driven to his death at the Wangyi Palace. The annals of the First Emperor bring the Qin line down to the surrender at Zhi Road and leave those scenes to the biography; the two accounts overlap and are not worded alike.
Shiji, Biography of Li Si; Basic Annals of the First Emperor of Qin
05 / 18 In the sources
Xiao He had been a chief clerk of Pei and had shielded Liu Bang from the law more than once while both were ordinary men. The biography says plainly what the archives were worth: after Xiang Yu burned the capital, the King of Han knew the empire's throat and its sores because Xiao He had taken the Qin documents whole. That is a historian's judgment, not a rumour: he is naming the cause of a conquest.
Shiji, Hereditary House of Chancellor Xiao
06 / 18 In the sources
The scene is in Zhang Liang's hereditary house, and it puts Fan Kuai's rebuff first: the dog butcher said go back to the open camp, and the Lord of Pei would not hear it. Zhang Liang then makes the argument out of the same cloth — a man come to cut down a scourge should arrive in plain white, and gao su is the phrase in the text, the undyed white silk of mourning and of no-office — and the hereditary house ends with a proverb already old when he used it, “helping Jie oppress”, the last king of the Shang. Sima Qian quotes the speech back at himself in the chapter's final judgment, which is how we know he thought it the best thing in the file.
Shiji, Hereditary House of Marquis Liu
07 / 18 In the sources
The summons and the three articles are in the annals of Gaozu, and Ban Gu copies them into the annals of Emperor Gaozu with small changes of wording; the sentence about the officers staying at their posts as before — “let every clerk remain at his post as he is” — appears in both, together with the promise that he had come to remove harm and not to commit violence. This is the most quoted sentence in the history of Han, and it was addressed to the head men of the Guanzhong counties in a single afternoon.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Emperor Gaozu; Hanshu, Annals of Emperor Gaozu
08 / 18 Debated
The annals add the line that ends the episode's argument: the people rejoiced all the more, and feared only that Liu Bang would not be made King of Qin. Whether the three articles were ever actually enforced is another question and a good one — the codes Xiao He compiled for the new dynasty follow the Qin classifications so closely that the excavated statutes found at Zhangjiashan in Hubei, copied out in 186 BCE, still carry Qin's punishments by name. On the most likely reading, “all the rest of the Qin law is void” described a moment of occupation, not a legal system; the statute book of early Han was Qin's, tidied.
Zhangjiashan Han tomb bamboo slips, Statutes and Ordinances of the Second Year (tomb 247, Jiangling), dated 186 BCE; Hanshu, Treatise on Punishments and Laws
09 / 18 In the sources
The informer is named in the annals of Xiang Yu: Cao Wushang, Liu Bang's left commander. The same pages give the odds without rhetoric — a hundred thousand at Bashang, four hundred thousand at Xiji and Hong Gate — and then the order that the men be feasted at dawn, which was the attack.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
10 / 18 Commentary
Fan Zeng of Juchao, whom Xiang Yu had raised to the place of Second Father, is the earliest of the age's professional planners, and the annals give his reading in one argument and one order: the ambition is not small, so strike. Sima Qian reports the cloud-gazing without comment, in a chapter otherwise built out of what men said and did; a reader who wants the episode without the omen has to take the omen out by hand, which is a preference, not a finding. The order to attack belongs to the same speech.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
11 / 18 In the sources
Xiang Bo was Xiang Yu's uncle, his father's youngest brother, and had once committed a killing and been sheltered by Zhang Liang; the two had been on visiting terms ever since, says the hereditary house. That private debt is the hinge of the whole night, and the text does not dress it up: Xiang Bo came because of it, and not for anyone's good. Zhang Liang's refusal to slip away is given as his own reason — he was under orders to attend the Lord of Pei for the King of Han, and to vanish now would be unrighteous and unutterable.
Shiji, Hereditary House of Marquis Liu; Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
12 / 18 In the sources
The apology is given at length: they had worked together to break Qin, he had not expected to be the one who got in first, and now some petty man's words had put a gap between them. The particulars of the scene — the hundred and odd riders, the cup of long life, the marriage bond sworn with Xiang Bo — are Sima Qian's, in the annals of Xiang Yu; Ban Gu retells the interview in the annals of Emperor Gaozu, much shortened.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu; Hanshu, Annals of Emperor Gaozu
13 / 18 In the sources
The signal gets no answer, so the old man gets up, walks out of the middle gate of the camp, and calls the van of the army to him: “the Lord of men is not hardened in his nature; go in and drink to him, and when the drinking is over ask to dance with a sword before the company, and strike Liu Bang in his seat and kill him. If you do not, you will all be taken by him.” The annals of Xiang Yu put that speech in Fan Zeng's mouth and then let the table go on drinking.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
14 / 18 In the sources
“Xiang Zhuang came up with his sword drawn and danced, and kept pressing at Liu Bang; Xiang Bo also drew and danced, and often spread his body like a wing over him.” Then Zhang Liang went out at the gate of the camp to fetch Fan Kuai, and the annals give the exchange in three lines: how is it going today? — desperate: Xiang Zhuang has come up with his sword and dances, and his intent is always on your lord. It is Fan Kuai who answers with the sentence that sends him in: “this is close — let me go in and share his fate.” The idiom “Xiang Zhuang dances with his sword, his intent always on Liu Bang” comes out of this paragraph and nowhere else in early writing.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
15 / 18 In the sources
The entrance is one of the great set pieces of the Shiji and every detail above is in it: the shield slung on the shoulder, the guard of crossed halberds struck down, the tent curtain pushed aside, the man standing facing east with his hair lifting and his eyes nearly split. Xiang Yu's hand went to his sword and he knelt upright — the posture of a man expecting a rush — and asked what guest this was. Zhang Liang answered: the chariot-partner of Liu Bang, named Fan Kuai.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
16 / 18 In the sources
“A man who cannot refuse death would not haggle over a bowl of wine” is how Fan Kuai opens, and the annals give the speech in full — the oath, the sealed treasuries, the army drawn back out of the pass to wait, the slander being listened to instead of the service. Fan Kuai had been a dog butcher of Pei and had married a sister of Liu Bang's wife; the histories keep both facts and never turn either into a joke.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
17 / 18 In the sources
The reproach ends with the phrase that decided the day: “this is the continuation of Qin's downfall.” The annals answer it in three characters — Xiang Yu had nothing to give, and said “sit”. Cao Wushang's name is not raised at the table; the informer was killed afterwards, in his own camp, on Liu Bang's return.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
18 / 18 In the sources
The escape and the broken cup close the scene in the annals of Xiang Yu, and the murder of Cao Wushang follows in the same pages. Xiang Yu then entered the capital, put the surrendered King of Qin to death, burned the palaces — the histories say the fire lasted three months — and divided the realm into eighteen kingdoms, calling himself overlord of Western Chu. The man he had spared at the feast was sent off to Ba, Shu and the valley of the Han with three Qin kings planted in front of him to keep the pass shut; on the way there the army burned the plank roads behind it, says Zhang Liang's hereditary house — the standard way of looking like a man who intended to stay where he had been put. The valley gave him his title, and the title gave the empire its name for four centuries.
Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu; Basic Annals of Emperor Gaozu; Hereditary House of Marquis Liu

In this episode

Seven names carried 206 BCE: the rebel who took the capital, the warlord who had been promised it, and five men whose advice decided which of the two was going to be a founder.

Liu Bang劉邦
256–195 BCE
Village official of Pei, fifty years old when he took the capital. Held the pass three months on a promise, and won an empire by refusing what he was offered.
Xiang Yu項羽
232–202 BCE
Commander of the allied armies, forty thousand strong. Broken the Qin main body already, he came west to punish a man he had agreed to make king.
Zhang Liang張良
fl. 218–195 BCE
Heir of a destroyed state, counselor to both men in turn. Persuaded his general to sleep in the open and later walked him out of a banquet.
Fan Kuai樊噲
d. 189 BCE
A dog butcher of Pei by trade, brother-in-law to Liu Bang. Remonstrated with his lord at the palace and carried a shield through the gate at Hong Gate.
Xiao He蕭何
d. 193 BCE
Liu Bang's old superior in the county office. While the generals scrambled for gold he loaded the archives: maps, registers, statutes.
Fan Zeng范增
d. 204 BCE
An old man of Juchao, the strategist behind Xiang Yu's throne. Read Liu Bang's abstinence as ambition, and cursed the feast afterwards.
Ziying子嬰
d. 206 BCE
Forty-six days King of Qin. He killed the minister who had enthroned him, then came out in a plain cart with a cord about his neck.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Ziying was the last emperor of Qin.

What the sources say

He was the last king. When Zhao Gao made him head of the house in late 207 he took away the imperial style and called him King of Qin, and the histories keep that title for his forty-six days; the seals he carried out at Zhi Road were the seals of an empire he had been demoted from.

Shiji, Basic Annals of the First Emperor of Qin; Hanshu, Annals of Emperor Gaozu
The story you may know

The three articles were the beginning of Han law.

What the sources say

They were the terms of an occupation, and short-lived as such. The codes Han actually ran on grew out of the Qin statutes Xiao He carried out of the archives: the traditional nine chapters keep Qin's seven and add household, corvée and stables. The bamboo statutes copied out in 186 BCE and found at Zhangjiashan read like Qin law with the worst edge taken off, not like three articles.

Hanshu, Treatise on Punishments and Laws; Jinshu, Treatise on Punishments and Laws; Zhangjiashan Han tomb slips, tomb 247
The story you may know

Xiang Yu lost the empire at Hong Gate because he was too honourable to kill a rival.

What the sources say

The histories give a smaller reason and a larger one. The smaller: he had just taken Liu Bang's submission in front of his own officers, and Fan Zeng's signal was looked at and not answered. The larger: the settlement he made three months later — eighteen kingdoms, himself as overlord of Western Chu, Liu Bang parked behind three Qin kings in a valley — is what had to be lost. One banquet did not decide a realm, and Sima Qian, who loved the scene, says as much by setting the partition out in full.

Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu; Basic Annals of Emperor Gaozu
The story you may know

The cloud-gazing proved that Liu Bang had heaven on his side.

What the sources say

It is in the text — Fan Zeng says he sent men to look — but it is one omen among the several Sima Qian plants round the rise of Han, and none of them is evidence for anything except belief in them. What the surrounding sentences show is a general reading his opponent's changed behaviour and drawing the obvious conclusion: a man who wants nothing yet wants a great deal.

Shiji, Basic Annals of Xiang Yu
The story you may know

Zhang Liang was a cool, otherworldly strategist with no body of his own.

What the sources say

His hereditary house says he was sickly most of his life, that after the empire was settled he took to breath-cultivation and stopped eating grain, and that he shut his door and did not go out for over a year. He also asked for the smallest fief on offer, the place where he had first met Liu Bang, and explains the request in the text as a piece of self-preservation. The immortal in the mountain cap is the novel's doing.

Shiji, Hereditary House of Marquis Liu

Sources

Every caption and quotation in this episode traces to one of these. Where scholars disagree, the panel says so.

  • Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji) — Sima Qian c. 91 BCE Basic Annals of Emperor Gaozu; Basic Annals of the First Emperor of Qin; Basic Annals of Xiang Yu; Hereditary House of Marquis Liu (Zhang Liang); Hereditary House of Chancellor Xiao (Xiao He); Biography of Li Si. Sima Qian wrote about a century after these events, out of the court archives and what he called the scattered old reports he went and collected himself.
  • Book of the Han (Hanshu) — Ban Gu, finished by Ban Zhao 1st c. CE Annals of Emperor Gaozu: the compressed official version of the entry into the pass, the three articles and the Hong Gate interview, plus the Treatise on Punishments and Laws for what Han law really looked like.
  • Book of the Jin (Jinshu) — Fang Xuanling et al. 648 CE Treatise on Punishments and Laws: the later legal tradition's own account of how the nine chapters of Han were made out of Qin's seven, and of the three articles as a moment, not a code.
  • Zhangjiashan Han Tomb Bamboo Slips (Tomb 247, Jiangling, Hubei) — Wenwu Chubanshe, excavation report 2001; revised reading 2006 The statutes and ordinances copied out in the second year of Empress Lü (186 BCE) and buried with an official of Nanjun: the closest thing we have to Han law as it actually ran in the first generation after the three articles.
  • The Early Chinese Empires: Qin and Han — Michael Loewe 2007 The standard English survey of the two empires, used here for the chronology of the years 209–206 BCE and for how far the surviving documents let a historian push the received story.
