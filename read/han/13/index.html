The Yellow Turbans

Jizhou and Yingchuan, 184 CE — A preacher of the Great Peace spent twelve years building an empire inside the empire with nothing but charmed water and a confession, fixed the day of its rising, set two of the emperor's own attendants to open the gates to him — and was betrayed by his own disciple, so that the house of Han grew weak that spring, before the rebels had fairly marched.

01 / 18 Note
The rising stands in the Later Han history in three places: the emperor's annal for the dates, the memoir of the general who broke the main host for the movement's inside story, and the eunuchs' memoir for the capital's half of it. The Tongjian relays all three and is used as a relay, not a witness — with one exception, the name of the movement's way, which is its own and not theirs.
Hou Han shu, juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling; juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 78, Memoir of the Eunuchs; Zizhi Tongjian, juan 58
02 / 18 In the sources
The kneeling and the confessing of offences are the Han memoir's own words, which the Tongjian copies, and so are the talisman-water and the recited formula; the memoir adds what mattered more, that the sick were cured often enough for people to talk. What is the Tongjian's, and not in the memoir at all, is the name — it calls the practice the way of Great Peace, where the memoir has him serving the way of the Yellow Emperor and Laozi — and its word for it, a sorcery by which he taught, which is judgment and not a checkable fact.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; Zizhi Tongjian, juan 58
03 / 18 Debated
The eight provinces are the memoir's list — Qing, Xu, You, Ji, Jing, Yang, Yan and Yu — the populated east and centre, with no frontier province in the west. The thirty-six and its sizes, the great ones over ten thousand men and the lesser six or seven thousand, are Huangfu Song's memoir, which the Tongjian copies; the emperor's annal counts the same thirty-six in men instead of divisions, at thirty-six ten-thousands all wearing yellow head-cloths, and cites a rival history for over thirty-six ten-thousands. So the books differ in what the thirty-six is a number of, and this page follows the memoir, which reads as the organisation. The figure for the proselytising is loose, ten-odd years, and this page rounds it to twelve. The word for a battalion, fang, means a square or a division, and the memoir glosses it as the title of a general: these were commands, not bands.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
04 / 18 In the sources
The white-earth writing, the day and the slogan are Huangfu Song's memoir's, which the Tongjian copies. The slogan is given here in translation only, and the memoir introduces it as a false report circulating, not a creed the movement was asked to believe: the Blue Heaven is dead, the Yellow Heaven is to stand, this year is jiazi, and there is great luck under the whole sky. The memoir says the yellow head-cloth was their mark, and the annal has the man style himself Yellow Heaven; neither book explains the colour. The reading of yellow as the virtue that succeeds the Han's fire is put elsewhere in the history into another great man's calculations, and is not this movement's.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
05 / 18 In the sources
That the arrangement reached Regular Attendants of the inner court is the point, and both sides tell it: Huangfu Song's memoir for the plot, with the men named as his inside men and the day agreed with them; and the memoir of the eunuchs for the wider fact that Zhang Rang and his fellows really had dealings with Zhang Jue, and for what the exposure cost Feng Xu and Xu Feng, who were discovered and put to death for it. That no bargain in money is recorded anywhere is worth noting against the usual telling. The junction at Ye and the gathering out of Jing and Yang are in the same sentence of the Han memoir, which the Tongjian copies rather than adds to.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 78, Memoir of the Eunuchs
06 / 18 In the sources
Tang Zhou stands in the record as the movement's own man, a disciple of Zhang Jue from Jinan, given no grievance, no price and no later career, so nothing is built around him. The memoir dates his letter to the year and the Tongjian to the season; neither gives it a month, and the arrests that follow are undated too. That the rising came in the second month, before the day that had been agreed, and that riders were sent out night and day to warn the divisions, is the memoir's own account of why it came early.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; Zizhi Tongjian, juan 58
07 / 18 In the sources
The dismemberment and the thousand-odd dead are in Huangfu Song's memoir, which the Tongjian copies; the emperor's annal does not record them at all, and its first entry for the year is the rising in the second month. What the annal does date is the rest of the court's answer, and it is worth having: the general-in-chief and the eight passes in the third month, and four days later the amnesty for the prohibited partisans, which the annal attributes to the Regular Attendant Lü Qiang's warning that men long under the bolt would join the Turbans and be past remedy — and the annal adds that one man was left out of it, Zhang Jue. That is the lifting this line's last episode ended on, and it is a war measure. The new reign-title and a general amnesty came in the twelfth month, when the main host had already been broken; no recorded clause let enrolled followers home unpunished.
Hou Han shu, juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling; juan 67, Memoir of the Partisan Prohibitions; juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song
08 / 18 In the sources
The burning of the offices, the provinces losing their footing and the flight of the chief officers are Huangfu Song's memoir's own words, which the Tongjian copies with the same sense; the annal supplies the two kingdoms that seized their kings and says they all rebelled on one day. Where the memoir says within ten days the Tongjian says within a month, and the memoir adds what no date shows, that they killed men to sacrifice to Heaven and were also called Ant Men, from the ant-hill. The two books do not say that the people feared the bandits and despised their own officials; that sentence is not in either, and a page should not lean on it.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
09 / 18 In the sources
The annal puts three of the four commands into a single sentence of the third month, immediately after the general-in-chief and the eight passes, and the Tongjian has the whole business in the same order. The titles of the fourth, Dong Zhuo, are in his biography, which also says he went out to supersede Lu Zhi. How two of them got their commands is recorded too: the four offices of the court recommended Lu Zhi, and the ministers of state recommended Zhu Jun for having a strategist's gifts. Huangfu Song had the title in his memoir while already a governor of Beidi, and was not, as this line's first draft had it, called back from being out of work.
Hou Han shu, juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling; juan 64, Memoir of Lu Zhi; juan 71, Memoirs of Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun; juan 72, Memoir of Dong Zhuo
10 / 18 In the sources
The defeat in Yingchuan and the holding of Changshe stand in Huangfu Song's memoir, which needs them to make its general's victory, and Zhu Jun's memoir in the same volume tells the same business from his side; the annal dates the defeat to the fourth month and the battle that relieved Changshe to the fifth. That Lu Zhi and Dong Zhuo campaigned against Zhang Jue without result and came back is stated in Huangfu Song's memoir, and that is why the court ordered Song north. Dong Zhuo's own biography gives the place of his defeat as Xiaquyang and says he was sent as Lu Zhi's substitute, and the annal says he did not prevail. Neither his biography nor Lu Zhi's says that Lu Zhi brought him out, and this page should not say it.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoirs of Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling; juan 72, Memoir of Dong Zhuo
11 / 18 In the sources
The fire at Changshe is in Huangfu Song's memoir with its particulars: the enemy's camp in grass, the wind that rose that night, the reed torches, the picked hands slipped out beyond the lines, the torches held up from the wall, the sally at the drums, and tens of thousands killed. The memoir says plainly that his troops were few, gives the speech in which he argues from the unexpected change rather than from numbers, and has him name Tian Dan, the general who saved Qi by fire from inside a besieged town — the annotation to the memoir spells the story out and sends it to the Shiji. The relief is named in the Han book itself, not first in the Tongjian: the emperor sent the Cavalry Commandant Cao Cao with troops, and Song, Cao Cao and Zhu Jun joined and fought again. The Tongjian adds Cao Cao's native state, and the month.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; Zizhi Tongjian, juan 58
12 / 18 In the sources
The advice, the mansion at Ye and the fifty million are in Huangfu Song's memoir; the prices and the haggling in the Western Garden are in the memoir of the eunuchs, and belong to the year after the rising, not to the rising, and the annal dates the same squeeze to a levy of ten cash per mu for the repair of the palaces. The emperor's sentence is given in the eunuchs' memoir as a habit of the reign, and the first term in it is a title of honour, not a father. When the capital's plot was found out the emperor called the Regular Attendants to account for their dealings with Zhang Jue and asked whether they were not ripe for beheading; they went bareheaded and barefoot, begged to be put in the emperor's own prison, offered their private wealth for the war, and were told by edict to put their caps back and attend as before. The officer who pressed for the heads of the Ten Regular Attendants had his letter handed to them to read, and died under interrogation in prison on a charge of studying the Yellow Turban way, worked up on their orders.
Hou Han shu, juan 78, Memoir of the Eunuchs; juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
13 / 18 In the sources
The whole of it is Zhu Jun's memoir: the refusal of the surrender because taking men in who fought when it suited them and craved terms when it did not would open the door to both, the two failed assaults, the mound he climbed to see the position, and the words he turns and says to his staff officer Zhang Chao, which are the words above, shortened here as they are shortened everywhere on this page. The memoir also reports what the stratagem did not settle — the surrendered Han Zhong was killed anyway by the commandery governor of Nanyang, who had long resented him, the rest were afraid, chose a new leader and came back into Wan, and had to be destroyed again — and that at the first of the two sieges the court came within one memorial of recalling Zhu Jun in disgrace, and was argued out of changing a general in the field by the Minister of Works. The counts are the memoir's own, written as credit and not as scandal.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Zhu Jun; Zizhi Tongjian, juan 58
14 / 18 In the sources
The fee refused, the report made by the man who was sent for it, the emperor's anger and the prison-cart are in Lu Zhi's memoir, and the annal tells the same business with its own two sentences: the eunuchs slandered him and he took his punishment, and it names the Yellow Gate man. Lu Zhi is given no speech anywhere in this episode — the words quoted above are the envoy's, and the memoir reports them as what he told the emperor. The memoir's sequel is the part usually left out: Huangfu Song, called north after Lu Zhi and Dong Zhuo had both come back without result, took Guangzong, and then praised Lu Zhi's methods of command to the court and said that whatever he had himself achieved he had drawn on Zhi's planning. Lu Zhi was made a Master of Documents again in that same year.
Hou Han shu, juan 64, Memoir of Lu Zhi; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
15 / 18 In the sources
The two brothers' deaths, the opened coffin and the mound stand in Huangfu Song's memoir, which gives the mound and its count as its general's credit, sets it south of the town, and glosses such a mound as corpses piled and sealed over with earth; the annal dates the coffin to the tenth month and adds the place the head was sent on to, the horse-market, where such things were shown. Nothing in either book says that men who surrendered at Xiaquyang were killed; a surrender killed after terms is recorded, but further south, at Wan, and by a commandery governor serving in the same siege. Where these books give tens of thousands for one battle, or a hundred thousand for a place, the figures are the source's claim and are stated here as that.
Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
16 / 18 In the sources
Two of the three, and not the third. Lu Zhi's memoir has the envoy make the report and the annal say the eunuchs slandered him; Huangfu Song's memoir names the accusers, the confiscated mansion at Ye and the money he would not hand over, and gives the charge as fighting without result and spending much — a claim to distrust about a courtier, though the two tell it alike and state the spite. Zhu Jun is the odd one out: nothing was alleged against him but slowness, no eunuch is named, and the man who saved his command was the Minister of Works on the plain argument that changing a general in the field is what war fears. All three memoirs also record the honours that came first, and the annal keeps entering Yellow Turban bands in province after province down to the last year of the reign.
Hou Han shu, juan 64, Memoir of Lu Zhi; juan 71, Memoirs of Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
17 / 18 In the sources
The two opening sentences of the panel are ours and not the record's; the record supplies the fact underneath them. Liu Yan's proposal is in his memoir, and so is why it took effect when it did: the inspector of Yizhou was complained of far away, and two other inspectors had been killed by rebels. The memoir gives his next move too — he had asked for a province in the far south to be out of the way, and went out instead as an envoy overseeing the armies with the governorship of Yizhou, along with a governor of Yu and a governor of You, each kept at his old rank. The book's verdict on the change is eight characters, zhou ren zhi zhong, zi ci er shi: the weight of provincial command dates from here. The annal's verdict on the year of the rising is four, han sui wei ruo, the house of Han thereupon grew weak — and it lifts that out of a treatise, not out of its own narrative.
Hou Han shu, juan 75, Memoir of Liu Yan; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling
18 / 18 Commentary
This is where the line stops, and the stopping is deliberate: 189 and everything in it belong to another telling, and this page has quoted nothing from them. What our four histories leave standing at the end of 188 is the ledger above — provinces with governors who could raise men and hold grain, an inner court that had survived being blamed for the war, a gentry that had been let back in on condition of fighting, and three officers whom the court they saved had arrested, disgraced, or within an ace of recalling.
Hou Han shu, juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling; juan 64, Memoir of Lu Zhi; juan 71, Memoirs of Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun

In this episode

Seven people carried 184 CE: a faith-healer who built a state inside the state, the disciple who told on him, the three generals who were handed the war, and the imperial consort who was murdered over it.

Zhang Jue張角
d. 184 CE
The healer of Julu who styled himself Great Excellent Teacher, da xianliang shi, and served the way of the Yellow Emperor and Laozi, later called the way of Great Peace. Ten-odd years of charmed water and confession made him head of an enrolled empire inside the empire; he died of sickness before it was broken.
Ma Yuanyi馬元義
d. 184 CE
Zhang Jue's chief of one great battalion, who gathered tens of thousands out of Jing and Yang for a junction at Ye, came and went to the capital and took two Regular Attendants at the emperor's elbow as his men inside it, and was torn apart by teams at Luoyang when a fellow disciple gave the whole arrangement away.
Huangfu Song皇甫嵩
d. 195 CE
Left Commandant of the Guards, sent west into Yingchuan with Zhu Jun. He won that war in one night's fire and the north in a dawn assault, and in the year after, an accusation from two Regular Attendants he had offended took his general's seal and six thousand of his households.
Zhu Jun朱儁
d. 195 CE
Right Commandant of the Guards, fighting his own war at Wan. The one officer who argues that men with no way out must fight to the death — and who refused a surrender for that reason, took the place by storm, and is credited with the killing either way.
Lu Zhi盧植
d. 192 CE
North Commandant of the Guards, leading the northern army's five campuses, and the age's great teacher of the classics. He would not fee the Yellow Gate envoy sent to his camp, was reported for it by the man himself, and lost his command and very nearly his life.
Zhang Rang張讓
d. 189 CE
Regular Attendant, the man the emperor said was his lord. When the capital's plot was found out the emperor turned on the Regular Attendants, and Zhang Rang and his fellows went bareheaded and barefoot, paid their private wealth into the war, and were left in office; the officer who pressed for their deaths died in prison on a charge worked up against him.
Liu Yan of Yi劉焉
d. 194 CE
Imperial clansman who in 188 proposed turning the regional inspectors into governors with real power, then asked for such a post himself — a far southern province, to be out of the way — and got Yizhou instead.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

The Yellow Turbans were the opening enemy of the Three Kingdoms heroes, who made their names fighting them.

What the sources say

The war of 184 was fought and won by the dynasty's own career officers — Lu Zhi, Huangfu Song, Zhu Jun, and twice a defeated Dong Zhuo. The future founders are on the record only as junior men: Cao Cao arrives with the relief at Changshe as a cavalry commandant, and Sun Jian carries the wall at Wan as a subordinate commander in Sima Guang's copy of Zhu Jun's account. Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei are nowhere in the record of 184; the peach-garden oath and the brothers' first battles against the Turbans belong to the fourteenth-century Romance of the Three Kingdoms, a novel and not a source.

Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoirs of Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun; Zizhi Tongjian, juan 58; Sanguo yanyi, a 14th-century novel, against them
The story you may know

The rising was a spontaneous explosion of desperate peasants in a bad year.

What the sources say

It was an enrolled organisation with a start of ten-odd years, a head-count, thirty-six divisional chiefs, an appointed day and men at court, and the histories explain the business as a bureaucracy leaking rather than a hunger boiling over. Nothing in the record makes 184 a year of famine; the year before it, and not the year of the rising, is the one the annal enters as having a great drought in the summer and an abundant harvest at its end.

Hou Han shu, juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling; Zizhi Tongjian, juan 58
The story you may know

The rebellion was crushed in 184 and the dynasty was rescued by the victory.

What the sources say

The leadership was destroyed in one autumn and the movement was not: the annals keep entering Yellow Turban bands in province after province for the rest of the reign. What the victory bought was a precedent — the province that feeds its own army and the governor with real troops — and on the reform that made the governorships permanent the Later Han history's own sentence is that the weight of provincial command dates from there.

Hou Han shu, juan 8, Annals of Emperor Ling; juan 75, Memoir of Liu Yan
The story you may know

The Taiping way was a scripted religion, and the Classic of Great Peace was the rebels' book.

What the sources say

The histories name no book of the movement's own — a way, a talisman, water, a recited formula, a confession, a day and a colour. But they do not leave it bookless: the memoir of Xiang Kai records how Gong Chong presented, under Emperor Shun, the divine book his master had got at a spring, a hundred and seventy juan called the Register of Great Peace in the Clean Series, how the offices refused it as demon-spoken and irregular and had it put away, and then adds that Zhang Jue came to have a good deal of that book. What is modern and contested is the next step: that the surviving Classic of Great Peace is that book, and so the rebels' scripture.

Hou Han shu, juan 30 lower, Memoirs of Lang Yi and Xiang Kai; juan 71, Memoir of Huangfu Song; modern scholarship, contested
The story you may know

The empire rewarded the generals who put the rising down.

What the sources say

Two of the three men this page names as its saviours were struck afterward and the third very nearly was. Lu Zhi lost his command in a prison-cart for refusing to fee an envoy and was condemned, off by one degree. Huangfu Song, in the year after he broke the main host, had his general's seal seized and six thousand households struck off his marquisate on a charge his own memoir says two Regular Attendants made up out of spite. Zhu Jun was saved from recall at Wan only by a colleague's memorial. All three had been enfeoffed and raised first; the years after 184 read as a court settling accounts with its officers, not thanking them.

Hou Han shu, juan 64, Memoir of Lu Zhi; juan 71, Memoirs of Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun
