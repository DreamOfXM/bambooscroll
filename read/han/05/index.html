The Great Hammer

The Mobei, 119 BCE — Two cavalry armies of fifty thousand each crossed the desert with hundreds of thousands of men hauling behind them; on the record's own tally they brought in eighty-nine thousand heads, and fewer than thirty thousand of the hundred and forty thousand horses counted out at the frontier gate came back.

01 / 17 Note
These pages are built from four books and nothing else: the Records of the Grand Historian, whose chapter on the two generals was written by Sima Qian, an official of this same court within a generation of the events; the Book of the Han, compiled about a century later by Ban Gu and his family, which reworks that chapter and adds the career totals; the Book of the Later Han, for the later centuries; and the Zizhi Tongjian, which arranges the reign year by year and is used here for sequence. The treatise on food and money in the Book of the Han supplies the bill in the last panels. No novel, opera, drama or film is used as a source for any scene here; where one of them disagrees with the record, the Myth Check at the end says so.
On our sources
02 / 17 In the sources
In the fourth year of Yuanshou, spring: the emperor ordered the General-in-Chief Wei Qing and the Brilliant Rider Huo Qubing to lead fifty thousand cavalry each, with transferring infantry numbering several hundred thousand following at the army's heels, and “the men who dared to fight hard and go deep were all attached to the Brilliant Rider.” The council sentence that precedes it is the one above, and it is the emperor's own reading of his old enemy's adviser. That second army had no subordinate generals at all; Huo Qubing used Li Gan and the rest as senior colonels doing division commanders' work. The figures are the record's own and are written as claims, not as an audit.
Shiji, juan 111, Biographies of the General and the Brilliant Rider
03 / 17 In the sources
Zhao Xin's half is in the account of the Xiongnu and in the generals' chapter alike: once the Han troops have crossed the desert, their men and horses will be spent, and the Xiongnu can sit and collect their prisoners. On the strength of that reading the Chanyu moved all his baggage far to the north and waited north of the desert with all his picked troops. The emperor's counter-reading — that massing infantry would answer it — is in the same passage, one clause behind it. A war of supply, with both sides reading the same ledger.
Shiji, juan 111; compare juan 110, Account of the Xiongnu
04 / 17 In the sources
Two clauses carry it: the Brilliant Rider was originally to go out of Dingxiang against the Chanyu; captured prisoners said the Chanyu was east; therefore the Brilliant Rider was ordered out of Dai Commandery and Wei Qing out of Dingxiang. Wei Qing's four column commanders are named in the chapter — Li Guang as Forward General, the Grand Master of the Imperial Stables as Left General, Zhao Yiqi as Right General and Cao Xiang, Marquis of Pingyang, as Rear General — and Gongsun Ao, newly stripped of his marquisate, was serving with him as Central General. All of them belonged to his army by the emperor's arrangement, because he had no standing division commanders of his own.
Shiji, juan 111; juan 109 for Gongsun Ao's rank
05 / 17 In the sources
The ring is in the text as a four-character phrase, wu gang che zi huan wei ying: the armoured chariots making a camp of themselves, through which the cavalry was loosed. That is all the chapter says about how it was built, and it says it in one breath with the attack that went out through it. What followed is a day of meeting battle on ground the Xiongnu had chosen, and the numbers in it are the record's own claims.
Shiji, juan 111
06 / 17 In the sources
The sentence is short and it is the whole battle: a great wind rising, sand and gravel striking the faces, the two armies unable to see each other, Han loosing more horse on its left and right wings to encircle the Chanyu. The Chanyu judged that the Han troops were numerous and that their men and horses were still strong, and that the fight was going against him; so at dusk he took a carriage drawn by six mules, with a few hundred picked cavalry, and broke straight out through the encirclement to the northwest.
Shiji, juan 111
07 / 17 In the sources
The tally for his whole command on the return is nineteen thousand heads, plus the city and its burnt grain. The steppe's own week is the more eloquent record: the Xiongnu lost touch with the Chanyu for more than ten days, the Right Guili King heard the news and proclaimed himself Chanyu, and only when the real one came back at the head of his host did the claimant drop the title. An empire's worth of ceremony, dissolved in a fortnight of bad communications.
Shiji, juan 111
08 / 17 In the sources
Li Guang had begged for this battle. He said that he had fought the Xiongnu since he first tied up his hair and that now, for once, he had his chance at the Chanyu, and asked to lead the van and be the first to die against him. He was moved onto the eastern road anyway, whose grass and water were too poor to keep the column concentrated. Sima Qian states the reason without varnish: the emperor had given secret instruction that Li Guang was old and had the luck against him, and must not be allowed to face the Chanyu; and Wei Qing wanted Gongsun Ao, newly stripped of his marquisate, at that meeting for his own reasons. The order to report for questioning is quoted word for word.
Shiji, juan 109, Biographies of General Li of Longxi; juan 111
09 / 17 In the sources
The speech and the suicide are in Li Guang's biography, and so is the end of the other man on that road: Zhao Yiqi was handed over to the officials, sentenced to death as a matter of law, and redeemed himself as a commoner. Nothing in the record punishes Wei Qing for the order; after the battle he wanted the army's circumstances reported to the emperor, and it was in that connection that he summoned Li Guang to the marquee for the accounting. Sima Qian says only that he saw the man himself — diffident as a countryman, unable to put a sentence together — and he writes the death as humiliation rather than as guilt. The same biography carries the sequel: the son Gan, resenting the General-in-Chief for it, wounded him and was hushed up, and was then shot dead by Huo Qubing at a hunt in the imperial grounds, with the court told that a stag did it.
Shiji, juan 109, Biographies of General Li of Longxi
10 / 17 Debated
The emperor's own citation of merits survives as a quoted edict: he led the trained fighters he had taken, travelled light and provisioned thin, crossed the great desert, took the Zhangqu, killed Bicheji, swung round and struck the Left Great General, carried off drums and banners, crossed the Gouwu, and took three kings with eighty-three generals, chancellors, lieutenant-kings and commandants; he “sealed the summit of Mount Langjuxu, made the opposite offering at Guyan, and climbed to the Hanhai.” The tally is seventy thousand and four hundred and forty-three heads. The two histories differ, in this one sentence, about his own losses: the Records say his army was reduced by three in ten; the Book of the Han says two. They differ about his depth too — the Records bring his army out of Dai and Right Beiping more than a thousand li, the Book of the Han more than two thousand.
Shiji, juan 111; Hanshu, juan 55, Joint Biography of Wei Qing and Huo Qubing
11 / 17 In the sources
The bill is itemised: Lu Bode, who was met at the trysting place and did not miss the day, got sixteen hundred households and the title Fuli; a frontier commandant who took a king, twelve hundred; two kings of the surrendered men, thirteen hundred and eighteen hundred; two marquises already serving under him, three hundred apiece; the colonel Gan — Li Guang's son, who took the Left King's drums and banners and went on to hold his father's old post of Commander of the Guards — made a Marquis Within the Passes with two hundred households, and another colonel given a high rank of nobility with no fief in it. The record adds in the same breath that officers and common soldiers were made officials and that the gifts of reward were very great, and that the General-in-Chief gained no increase of fief, and that none of his officers, clerks or soldiers were made marquises.
Shiji, juan 111
12 / 17 In the sources
“Of the two armies going out of the pass, the pass inspection gave one hundred and forty thousand horse, public and private; and those entering the pass again were not full thirty thousand.” Better than three parts in four of the emperor's horse army, gone in one season — dead, foundered, left on the grass. The sentence is placed in Sima Qian's text immediately before the creation of the two Grand Marshal posts, which is how the chapter itself sets the price beside the reward.
Shiji, juan 111
13 / 17 In the sources
From this point the chapter dates the change in standing: Wei Qing retired further day by day, while Huo Qubing grew day by day more honoured, and most of Wei Qing's old friends and household men went off to serve the Brilliant Rider and got office and rank from it — “only Ren An would not.” The emperor had offered Huo Qubing instruction in the Sunzi and the Wuqi and was told that what matters is a commander's own methods, and that he need not study the older manuals of war; he had a house built for him and shown to him, and got the answer in the bubble above. Both sayings are in the same paragraph, and the paragraph ends: from this the emperor loved and prized him all the more.
Shiji, juan 111
14 / 17 In the sources
Huo Qubing had grown up in the palace and never learned to care for men: the imperial kitchen sent tens of cartloads of provisions after his army, and on the way home the overloaded carts threw out leftover grain and meat while there were starving soldiers; outside the passes, when men lacked food and could not stand up, he was still marking out a cuju pitch and playing on it — “there were many doings like this.” The other side of the pair is Su Jian, who had served under Li Guang and now rode in Wei Qing's camp, asking why the General-in-Chief did not recommend men and take disciples while he had the power. The answer above is given in full in the chapter, which adds that Huo Qubing took the same line in his own manner: “he was of this mind too, and that was how he led.” Sima Qian's judgment on Wei Qing is one of the sharpest sentences in the book: a man of good and yielding nature, who won the emperor by being soft, “and yet there was no one in the empire who praised him.”
Shiji, juan 111
15 / 17 In the sources
The record of the deaths after it is a catalogue. Huo Qubing's son held the marquisate six years and died in 110 with no heir, and the fief was struck out. After Huo Qubing's death, Wei Qing's eldest son lost the Marquisate of Yichun on a statutory fault; five years later his two brothers, marquises of Yin'an and Fogan, lost theirs over the wine-assessment — the quality of gold a marquis owed the ancestral temple. Two years after that the Champion Marquisate was extinguished. Four years later Wei Qing himself died, and the Marquisate of Changping went to his son, and was lost six years after that. Every Wei marquisate was gone within a generation of the victory. Ban Gu gathers the whole thing into one sentence at the end of the chapter: the Wei house rose with the General-in-Chief's first enfeoffment, five of his kin were made marquises, and in twenty-four years all five fiefs were taken away — “and in the Zhenghe era, when the Crown Prince was defeated, the Wei house was exterminated.”
Shiji, juan 111; Hanshu, juan 55
16 / 17 In the sources
The treatise on food and money dates it from the campaign: the year after the great strike, rewards and gifts came to five hundred thousand jin of gold; dead army horses were over a hundred thousand; the cost of transport, grain-haulage and equipment was not even counted in. “At this time the treasury was empty, and the fighting men rather went without their pay.” What the same pages list next: the seventeen grades of the saleable military-merit titles; the assessment on trade capital and the informers who enforced it; the salt and iron monopolies; the change to the five-zhu coin; over seven hundred thousand poor removed west of the passes and to fill the Shin Qin region beyond Shuofang, whose food and clothing fell on the state until “the treasury was greatly emptied.” These are the treatise's own numbers and its own list of consequences.
Hanshu, juan 24, Treatise on Food and Money
17 / 17 Debated
“After the General-in-Chief encircled the Chanyu, fourteen years, and he died; and in the end there were no further strikes against the Xiongnu, because Han's horses were few, and just then the two Yue were being destroyed to the south, Chaoxian attacked to the east, and the Qiang and the peoples of the southwest — so for a long time the Hu were not attacked.” A generation of readers has taken the Mobei as the knockout blow; the chapter that describes it says the empire could not do it again and did not try, and that the enemy came back into the border commanderies in the following decades. Ban Gu's obituary of the reign, at the end of the Annals of Emperor Zhao, gives the human price in one claim: inheriting the remainder of Emperor Wu's luxury and weariness and his wars, “within the seas was emptied and wasted, and household registrations were reduced by half.” That is a historian's judgment, not a surviving census return.
Shiji, juan 111; Hanshu, juan 7, Annals of Emperor Zhao, the final comment

Five people carried 119 BCE — the two generals who rode separate columns into one desert, the emperor who paid for the crossing, and the two men who counted what returned.

Wei Qing衛青
d. 118 BCE
A marquis's household servant who called himself a slave's son, and rose to General-in-Chief. Formed his waggons into a ring, fought the Chanyu in a sandstorm, and never again received an increase of his fief.
Huo Qubing霍去病
d. 117 BCE
Son of the empress's elder sister and nephew of his own commander, a palace attendant at eighteen. Given the picked men and no subordinate generals; buried with a tomb shaped like the mountains he took.
Emperor Wu劉徹
156–87 BCE
Built the horse herds for twenty years to lift this one hammer, and paid for it in the currency, the patents and the census of his own reign.
Li Guang李廣
d. 119 BCE
The old frontier shot, past sixty and with the luck against him, given the long road east instead of the Chanyu. He lost the way, and refused to be written up by a clerk.
Ren An任安

An officer in Wei Qing's household who would not change masters when the younger general rose. Twenty-eight years later he held the imperial credential in his hand and closed a gate on it.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Huo Qubing led a handful of light horse deep into the steppe and lived off the land, needing no supply train.

What the sources say

The record gives him fifty thousand cavalry and a baggage equal to the General-in-Chief's army, and says explicitly that the troops who dared to fight deep were all assigned to him. “Provisioned on the enemy” is in the emperor's own citation of his merits, and so is “marching extraordinarily far, with his provisions not cut off” — which means there was a line behind him. The strike was heavy, organised and enormously expensive, not a raiding party.

Shiji, juan 111; Hanshu, juan 55
The story you may know

The Mobei campaign captured the Chanyu and broke the Xiongnu as a power.

What the sources say

The Chanyu got away at dusk in a sandstorm with a few hundred horse, and the Han found out the next morning. The Xiongnu did not even know he had escaped for ten days — long enough for a king of the right wing to take his title. Nor was the power broken: the same history states that after 119 there were no more such strikes because Han was short of horses, and records Xiongnu raids into the frontier commanderies for years afterwards.

Shiji, juan 111; juan 110, Account of the Xiongnu
The story you may know

The sealing of Mount Langjuxu was a triumph at the true northern sea, Lake Baikal, and the ritual belongs to the campaign's single bloodless climax.

What the sources say

The three verbs are in the emperor's citation of merits: sealed Langjuxu, made the opposite offering at Guyan, climbed to the Hanhai. Where any of them are is still argued; the site of Langjuxu has been placed in the Khuit/Khanuy valley of Mongolia and elsewhere, and “Hanhai” has been read as Lake Baikal, as the Kerulen, and as a generic “great water.” The record fixes no distance, and the identification of a mountain from 119 BCE is a modern guess dressed as a fact.

Shiji, juan 111; Hanshu, juan 55 — the geography is nowhere specified in either
The story you may know

“The Xiongnu are not destroyed: there is no room for a household yet” proves Huo Qubing's saintly disinterestedness.

What the sources say

The line is genuine and preserved in the histories, and so is what Sima Qian puts in the same paragraph as its counterweight: the imperial kitchen's cartloads of grain and meat thrown away on the way home while soldiers went hungry, and the cuju ground he had marked out and played on in the field when his men lacked food and could not stand. The quotation and the rebuke come from the same two lines of text.

Shiji, juan 111
The story you may know

Wei Qing was pushed aside by court intrigue, and Huo Qubing's rise was his rival's triumph over his uncle.

What the sources say

The chapter knows a change of favour, not a quarrel between the two men: an added office for generals neither of whom could rank above the other, equal stipends, and most of Wei Qing's household men migrating to the better-paid younger one — Ren An the only named refuser. But there is blood between the houses, and it is in another chapter. Li Guang's son Gan, resenting the General-in-Chief for his father's death, attacked and wounded him, and Wei Qing hid the fact; some while later, at a hunt in the Ganquan palace grounds, Huo Qubing shot Gan dead, and the emperor concealed the killing — the record given out was that a stag butted him. Sima Qian puts no intrigue behind Wei Qing's standing; the reason he states for the decline is that the emperor's favour moved, day by day, to the nephew.

Shiji, juan 111; juan 109, Biographies of General Li of Longxi
