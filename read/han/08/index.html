Dead Beyond Ten Thousand Li

Chang'an and the Steppe, 53–33 BCE — A defeated claimant to the steppe throne rode south and called himself a subject of the Han; his brother rode west until he was killed by a Han colonel who had no authority to raise the troops that did it, and the capital then spent months arguing over whether to pay him.
18 panels~8 min read free to readevery line sourced
01 / 18 Note
This episode is told from the Book of the Han: the lower half of the Biography of the Xiongnu, the joint biography of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang, the biography of Xiao Wangzhi, and the annals of Emperor Xuan and Emperor Yuan. One passage on Wang Zhaojun is taken from the Book of the Later Han, and where the two Han histories differ about her the note says so. Her ballads and dramas are not used anywhere.
On our sources
02 / 18 In the sources
The emperor's own edict of 55 BCE gives the numbers: dead in the tens of thousands, eight or nine herds in ten lost, people starving and burning each other alive to get at grain. Ban Gu runs through the names in order — Tuqi, Hujie, Cheli, Wujie, Runzhen — each taking the title, several giving it up and joining somebody else's losing side.
Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower); juan 8, Annals of Emperor Xuan
03 / 18 In the sources
The grandees' reply is quoted at length: death in battle is the portion of a strong man, brothers fighting over a throne leave glory either way, the Han cannot swallow the Xiongnu in any case, and why overturn the institutions of the first men, serve Han, put the former chanyus to shame and become the laugh of every other state. Huhanye took the king's advice the same year, moved his people south to the wall and sent his son to the palace; Zhizhi sent a son of his own as well, and the Han received both.
Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower)
04 / 18 In the sources
He was set above every vassal king in the empire, and the edict gave the reason: the rites of the five thearchs and three kings do not reach that people, and my virtue cannot cover them, so let him be treated as a guest. The gift list survives item by item — seal and tassel, jade-fitted sword, bow and four arrows, one covered chariot, fifteen horses, twenty jin of gold, two hundred thousand cash, seventy-seven changes of clothing, eight thousand bolts of silk, six thousand jin of floss — and he stayed more than a month before being escorted out of the Ji Lu pass by sixteen thousand horse.
Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower); juan 8, Annals of Emperor Xuan
05 / 18 In the sources
Huang Ba and Yu Dingguo argued the order of the royal domain — capital first, then the states, then the barbarians — and wanted the rite of a vassal king. Xiao Wangzhi argued that a chanyu outside the calendar is a matching state by definition, that yielding the formal point puts the debt on the other side, and that if his descendants stop coming they will be absent, not rebellious. The emperor took the argument: the edict set the rank and the guest rite as Wangzhi asked, though in the ceremony the chanyu still called himself a subject.
Hanshu, juan 78, Biography of Xiao Wangzhi; juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower)
06 / 18 In the sources
Ban Gu's totals for the escort years come to thirty-four thousand hu of transported grain, and then, when Huhanye reported his people short of food in 48 BCE, twenty thousand hu more out of Yunzhong and Wuyuan. Sixteen thousand horse under Dong Zhong were left to guard his camp and to punish whoever would not submit to him; on his second audience the gifts were given again as before and then increased, and no more escort cavalry was sent, because a garrison was already there.
Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower)
07 / 18 In the sources
Huo Guang is entered as Grand Marshal General and Marquis of Bolu, of the Huo clan, and not by name; after him come Zhang Anshi, Han Zeng, Zhao Chongguo, Wei Xiang, Bing Ji, Du Yannian, Liu De, Liangqiu He, then Xiao Wangzhi tenth, alive and still in the room, and Su Wu eleventh. Ban Gu adds that Huang Ba, Yu Dingguo, Zhu Yi, Zhang Chang and others ended well and were famous in the reign, and were still left off the wall, which is how you can tell it was a selection. In the twelfth month of 49 BCE Emperor Xuan died, and Wangzhi took the will with the Junior Tutor Zhou Kan, under the emperor's kin on his mother's side, Shi Gao, Marquis of Leling, who was made Grand Marshal.
Hanshu, juan 54, Biography of Su Jian; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Xuan
08 / 18 In the sources
Hong Gong and Shi Xian had run the inner office since the previous reign, and Wangzhi had tried to replace them with gentlemen and lost. The order was sealed, given to a palace attendant with instructions to put it into Wangzhi's own hands, and the commandant's cavalry sent to ring his house as the summons came. His wife said the summons was not the emperor's meaning; his student Zhu Yun, a man of principle, urged him to die; and Wangzhi spoke to him by his courtesy name and asked for the poison at once. The emperor's answer is recorded in his biography: he struck his hands together and said he had guessed the man would not go to prison, and now he had killed his best tutor; the midday meal was found already served and he sent it back, and wept where the court could see him.
Hanshu, juan 78, Biography of Xiao Wangzhi
09 / 18 In the sources
He killed a rival who had already gathered fifty thousand, then went north and west: the Qijie, the Jiankun and the Dingling, three states absorbed and ruled from among them. Ban Gu puts the Jiankun seven thousand li east of the chanyu's court and five thousand li south of Cheshi, with the same care he gives every distance in the book. The move into Kangju that finished it was not a conquest: his people took to the cold and died on the road, and what arrived was worth about three thousand souls.
Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower); juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang
10 / 18 In the sources
Three missions in all were sent to Kangju to ask for the bodies of Gu Ji's party; each was detained, humiliated and refused any answer to the emperor's word, and then Zhizhi sent a message through the Protector General saying he was living in straits and wished to come and receive instructions, with a son to enter attendance. The commentator Yan Shigu's gloss on the letter says he wrote those words just to make game of the court, and Ban Gu's own summary is one line: he was as insolent and careless as this.
Hanshu, juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang; juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower)
11 / 18 In the sources
In 45 BCE the Imperial Secretary Gong Yu and the scholar Kuang Heng advised that the escort should stop at the wall, because Zhizhi's conversion was not yet sound and his court was unbearably far; Gu Ji's memorial argued the principle that the record calls holding them tied without pulling the knot, and offered to go as far as the chanyu's person. The Right General Feng Fengshi argued for sending him and the emperor agreed. Zhizhi was furious at the Han's leaning to his brother, and killed the embassy. No letter of Gu Ji's survives; what came back out of the city nine years later was the silk he had been carrying.
Hanshu, juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang
12 / 18 In the sources
Han Chang and Zhang Meng found Huhanye's people and herds grown thick, the game hunted out under the wall, and his great men urging him north; so they swore the oath on the east mountain of the Nuoshui, the chanyu stirring the wine with his stirrup knife and a gold share-plough, drinking from the bowl made from the skull of the Yuezhi king that Laoshang chanyu had broken. The ministers ruled that no envoy could bind the Han's descendants to a barbarian curse, and that the chanyu had now been given bad words to report to Heaven, and ordered the oath dissolved with a sacrifice; the emperor thought the offence small, let them pay it off, and left the oath standing.
Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower)
13 / 18 In the sources
The record names the crime and not the excuse: jiao zhi, forging the imperial edict, a capital charge. Tang's case was made before the army moved — Zhizhi is pressing on the Wusun and Dayuan, and if he takes them the walled states will be his in a few years; such a thing cannot be seen through by ordinary counsel, so the memorial will certainly fail. Yanshou thought the same and wanted to memorialise for it; Tang said the court and the grandees would never see so far, and Yanshou, wavering, gave no assent. Then he raised the columns anyway, and both men filed a memorial accusing themselves and describing the army on the march.
Hanshu, juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang
14 / 18 In the sources
The last day is written like a manual: a hundred-odd cavalry cantering under the wall, a hundred infantry in fish-scale order at the gate, drilling; the horse turned back by crossbows held at the full draw; the wooden palisade burnt in the night; past midnight the palisade broken and the defenders falling back onto the earth wall, shouting; the Kangju horse — ten thousand of them, in a dozen divisions ringing the city and answering those inside — charging the camp again and again in the dark and giving way each time; and at dawn fire on all four sides with gongs and drums shaking the ground, and the Kangju drew off. An arrow went through the chanyu's nose on the upper story; he went down fighting his way from room to room into the inner quarters, and died there of his wounds in the daylight. An acting second-in-command of a company named Du Xun took the head, and with it two Han envoys' staffs and the silk letters Gu Ji's party had carried. The chanyu's line above is the book's clause in his mouth: the Hanshu writes that he, reckoning the road long, judged the Han army could not endure a long attack, and it gives him no spoken words at all in this biography; the order to stay and fight from the city is ours, drawn from what he did.
Hanshu, juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang
15 / 18 In the sources
The sentence — hang it in the Haojie street among the foreign hostels, to show it ten thousand li, and make plain that whoever offends the strong Han, however far off, will be killed — is in a memorial, not in anybody's mouth. The Chancellor Kuang Heng and the Imperial Secretary Fan Yanshou objected that the heads had already been round every state, and that the Monthly Commands forbid exposing bones and rotting things in this season; the general Xu Jia answered with the meeting at Jiagu, where Kongzi had a buffoon who mocked a lord cut down and his head and feet carried out by different gates. Hang it ten days, the emperor ruled; the general is right.
Hanshu, juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang
16 / 18 In the sources
Liu Xiang, a prince of the blood who had been Director of the Imperial Clan, argued the deed against the emperor's own precedents: Chang Hui and Zheng Ji took land and titles for doing less, and the heavenly-horse war spent fifty thousand men, a hundred million in cash and four years to bring back thirty horses, and it got two marquises, three ministers and a hundred-odd second-rank officials enfeoffed or appointed, because it had been far away and the late emperor refused to count the faults. Gan and Chen had troubled no Han soldier and spent no dou of army grain, and were lying under an open case in front of the clerks who write accusations. The edict remitted their crime and told the ministers to settle the fief; Kuang Heng and Shi Xian replied that Zhizhi was a runaway who had stolen a title in a far land and not a real chanyu. The compromise was a marquisate for Yanshou and the title of Marquis Within the Passes for Tang, three hundred households each and a hundred jin of gold added, with a proclamation to the Supreme God and the ancestral temples and a general amnesty.
Hanshu, juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang
17 / 18 Debated
The annals are a line and a half: the era was changed to Jingning, and a Wang Qiang of the rear palace was given to the chanyu as a consort; the Xiongnu biography adds that she was a girl of good family from the palaces, styled Zhaojun. The Book of the Later Han, two centuries on, has the version everybody repeats — years in the palace without sight of the emperor, a request to be sent, a farewell audience where her beauty lit up the Han palace and the emperor regretted his gift and could not take it back. It also says she bore two sons and asked to go home when Huhanye died, and was told to keep barbarian custom; Ban Gu gives her one son and never mentions the request. Neither book has a painter, a portrait or a pipa.
Hanshu, juan 9, Annals of Emperor Yuan; juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower); Hou Han shu, juan 89, Biography of the Southern Xiongnu
18 / 18 Commentary
Hou Ying's ten arguments are mostly about Han people rather than about him: the Yin mountains as the Xiongnu's armoury, and the old men of the border who say the Xiongnu pass them weeping ever since they lost them; the wall as a hundred years of rock, timber and water-gates that no one generation could rebuild; and then the dependent-state descendants afraid to slip away, the poor children of soldiers who never came back, the border slaves who had heard that life north was easier, and the cornered criminals, all of them certain to go if the watchtowers thinned. The edict was one clause: stop discussing the abandonment of the frontier. Ban Gu closes the juan with what the settlement bought — three generations with no dog barking at the border — and with the rule it was run on: when they come, punish and contain them; when they go, be ready and hold them; keep the knot tied and let the fault be theirs. And he lived to write that Wang Mang cut the thread.
Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower)

In this episode

Six people carried the fifty years before Christ's birth turned into Han foreign policy: two brothers who each wanted the steppe throne, the courtier who received one of them as a subject, the colonel who killed the other without asking, and the woman who married into the peace they made.

Huhanye Chanyu呼韓邪單于
d. 31 BCE
Claimant in a five-cornered civil war who took his adviser's advice and gave the Han his name. Ended with a title above every vassal king, a Han bride, and a Han guard left at his camp.
Zhizhi Chanyu郅支單于
d. 36 BCE
His elder brother, who would not kneel and rode ten thousand li west instead. Built a city on the Talas, killed the envoys sent to talk to him, and was shot through the nose on the wall and died in his inner quarters.
Xiao Wangzhi蕭望之
d. 47 BCE
The grandee who argued that a chanyu should be received as a guest and not a subject. He won, was painted on the wall of honoured ministers while still alive, and was driven to poison two years later.
Gan Yanshou甘延壽
fl. 36–33 BCE
Protector General in the Western Regions, a frontiersman picked for his strength at throwing stones. He was ill in bed when his deputy raised an army in the emperor's name, and ended up riding at the head of it.
Chen Tang陳湯
fl. 47–36 BCE
Deputy commander: poor, always borrowing, disliked in his home district, twice in gaol. He forged the edict, the garrison colonies marched on his signature, and he spent the rest of his life defending it.
Wang Zhaojun王昭君
fl. 33–31 BCE
A woman of Nan commandery chosen into the palaces, given to the chanyu as a consort when the northern war was over. The era name was changed the same spring to mark the gift as finished.

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Wang Zhaojun was sent out because the emperor picked the wrong portrait, having been refused a bribe by the painter Mao Yanshou.

What the sources say

Neither Han history has a portrait bureau or a painter. The Book of the Han says only that a daughter of good family was given from the palaces; the Book of the Later Han says she asked to go and that the emperor saw her for the first time at the farewell. Mao Yanshou and the bribes enter with later anecdote collections, and the pipa is a stage property.

Hanshu, juan 9 and juan 94; Hou Han shu, juan 89; the anecdote tradition is later and is not a source for these years
The story you may know

Chen Tang's raid was the emperor's own plan, carried out without the paperwork.

What the sources say

The record says the opposite and is blunt about it: Gan Yanshou wanted to memorialise first, Chen Tang would not wait, and the edict was forged while the Protector General lay ill. Both men then accused themselves in writing on the day they marched. The argument over the skull, the months-long deadlock over the reward, and Tang's later imprisonment and degradation all follow from the fact that nobody had authorised any of it.

Hanshu, juan 70, Biographies of Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang
The story you may know

Huhanye's submission made the Xiongnu a Han province.

What the sources say

It made him a client in an unusually expensive class. He was ranked above every vassal king and was not administered as a subject; the Han sent grain, gold, silk and a guard, and asked for the words of his address, not for the government of his people. When he offered to take over the frontier wall himself, the court refused after one clerk listed what would walk out through the gap.

Hanshu, juan 94, Biography of the Xiongnu (lower); juan 78, Biography of Xiao Wangzhi
The story you may know

Zhizhi's city on the Talas was garrisoned by a lost Roman legion captured at Carrhae.

What the sources say

The hypothesis rests on one phrase in the Han account of the assault, describing infantry drilled in fish-scale order at the gate, plus a place-name. No Han source and no Roman source puts a legion east of the Jaxartes; the city is described as earth with a doubled wooden palisade, built with Kangju's help and conscripted labour. Raised in print by Homer Dubs and taken up since by a museum, the reading has not been accepted by specialists in Han military text.

Hanshu, juan 70 is the whole of the ancient evidence; the Roman legio IX and Carrhae are not connected to it
The story you may know

Wang Zhaojun drowned herself rather than marry the new chanyu.

What the sources say

The only early account of her after Huhanye's death says the reverse: she wrote asking to come home, Emperor Cheng told her to follow barbarian custom, and she married his successor and bore him two daughters. The river is later.

Hou Han shu, juan 89, Biography of the Southern Xiongnu

No number in this episode comes from it — the grain, gift and casualty totals are the Hanshu's own.
