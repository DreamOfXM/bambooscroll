The Road Bored Open

Longxi to the Oxus, 138 BCE — A palace officer volunteered for a mission to a people no Han man had reached, was held by the Xiongnu for more than ten of the thirteen years he was gone, reached the Oxus and failed to get an alliance there at all — and the geography he brought back turned the empire's west into a road.

01 / 18 Note
This line is told only from the official histories: the memoir of the peoples of the west in the Records of the Grand Historian, the Book of Han's memoir of Zhang Qian and Li Guangli and its treatise on the Western Regions, and the Book of the Later Han's own Western Regions chapter for what came after; the Zizhi Tongjian where it collects them. No novel, film or game is a source for a panel, and the phrase Silk Road is a nineteenth-century coinage, which is dealt with at the end.
On our sources
02 / 18 In the sources
The emperor was asking, the record says, because Han was set on destroying the Hu and this was the intelligence worth having. The memoir's own entry on the Yuezhi says where the cup came from: it was the Laoshang Chanyu, Modu's successor, who killed the Yuezhi king and made his skull into a drinking cup, after which the Yuezhi went far away, past Ferghana, and struck down the people of Daxia and settled on their land — the ones the Han envoy was sent to find. Neither history dates that killing, and the Yuezhi's old pasture between Dunhuang and the Qilian mountains is named only as what they lost. What the court did with such a report is stated flatly in the memoir: any road to the Yuezhi ran through Xiongnu country, so it advertised for a man who could get there. A palace attendant from Hanzhong named Zhang Qian answered the call.
Shiji, juan 123, Memoir of the Peoples of the West; Shiji, juan 110, Memoir of the Xiongnu; Hanshu, juan 61, Memoir of Zhang Qian and Li Guangli
03 / 18 Debated
The Chanyu's retort is the plainest statement of the geography of the age in either history, and it is in both. Then the sentence that decides the shape of the story: he was held ten-odd years, given a wife, had a son, and never let go of the Han credential. The histories give no year for the departure at all, and date the return only by the length of the absence, thirteen years. The Zizhi Tongjian places the whole story as a background note at the return, in the third year of Yuanshuo, 126 BCE, which is why the start is put at 139 or 138 BCE. Neither the 138 nor the 139 is the source's own dating.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 18
04 / 18 In the sources
The memoir says he slipped away west towards the Yuezhi with his own followers, and reached Dayuan, Ferghana, after some tens of days of riding. What filled the decade is not described; that he was trusted enough to be given a wife, and that the men of the steppe liked him, is as much as the record says, and it notes separately that Zhang Qian was a strong man, generous and trustworthy, and that the barbarians loved him for it. Tangyi Fu, the servant who had set out with him, is credited with keeping the party alive: a Xiongnu man and an excellent shot who, when there was nothing to eat, shot birds and beasts for the fire.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61
05 / 18 In the sources
The exchange is in both histories: the king of Dayuan had heard of Han's wealth and wanted the contact but could not get it; so he believed the envoy, sent him forward with conductors and interpreters to Kangju, and Kangju passed him on to the Great Yuezhi. This is the first recorded instance in the Chinese sources of the mechanism that would run the road for the next century: a chain of states, each providing the guide and the interpreter for the next stage. It is also the reason his report on Ferghana is so exact — he was its guest, and it wanted the trade.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61
06 / 18 Debated
The record of the refusal is a piece of political analysis rather than a snub: the Yuezhi's own king had been killed by the Hu and his heir set up in his place — the Book of Han says it was the dead king's wife who was made queen, a variant the Shiji does not carry — and having made the people of Daxia serve them and settled on their land, they were content, thought themselves far from Han, and had no wish at all to be avenged on the Xiongnu. Zhang Qian stayed with them over a year and, in the memoir's phrase, never got the handle of the matter. Everything the mission was for was written in that failure.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61
07 / 18 In the sources
The return is in the same two sentences in both books: going down along the southern range and trying to come out through the Qiang, he was taken by the Xiongnu again; held again for about a year; then the Chanyu died, and the Worthy King of the Left Guyli made war on the heir and took the title for himself — the Book of Han leaves that prince unnamed and says only that the country fell into disorder — and in that disorder Zhang Qian got home — with his Xiongnu wife and Tangyi Fu, and with nothing said about the son born to him there. The court made him Grand Master of the Palace, and gave his companion the title Lord Who Bore the Mission — a title, not a marquisate. Then comes the number that the historian sets down for the whole affair, and it is the only one of its kind in the chapter: when Zhang Qian set out there were a hundred-odd men with him, he was gone thirteen years, and only two of them came back.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 18
08 / 18 In the sources
The report is given as a list of places with their distances and their economies: Dayuan, said to be ten thousand li from Han, settled, ploughing rice and wheat, pressing grape wine, with walled houses, seventy-odd towns great and small and a population of some hundreds of thousands, and good horses which sweat blood, the offspring, the record says, of heavenly horses. He named the neighbours too: Kangju and Wusun and Yutian, the wandering ones and the settled ones, and to the east the salt marsh. He himself had seen four kingdoms — Ferghana, the Yuezhi, Bactria and Kangju — and reported five or six more at second hand. The wine and the alfalfa are the two crops the memoir says came back east out of it.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 96a, Treatise on the Western Regions
09 / 18 Commentary
The Han treatise on the Western Regions states the same hydrography and adds the measurements: the marsh, also called the Pool of Puchang, lies three or four hundred li across and a little over three hundred li from the Jade Gate and the Yang Gate, its water standing still, neither rising in summer nor falling in winter; and the common view, the treatise says, was that it travelled beneath the ground and came out again in the south, at the Jishi hills, as the river of China. Han envoys went on to trace the upper course and found jade-bearing mountains at the source, and the emperor checked his old books and named that mountain Kunlun. Sima Qian, who had the envoy's report in front of him, would not have the legend: since Zhang Qian traced the river's source, he writes, where is this Kunlun of the old records that anyone has seen? As for the monsters of the Classic of Mountains and Seas — I do not dare to speak of them.
Hanshu, juan 96a; Shiji, juan 123
10 / 18 In the sources
The inference and its consequence are in the memoir. Han sent secret missions out of Shu and Jianwei on four routes — by the passes of the Mang, the Ran, the Xi, and of the Qiong and Bo — each pushing a thousand or two thousand li, and the northern ones were closed by the Di and Zuo, the southern by the Xi and Kunming, who had no lords, liked raiding, killed the envoys and took what they carried, so that nothing got through. But they heard that a thousand-odd li further west there was an elephant-riding country called Dianyue, and that Shu traders who had slipped out on their own had reached it; and in that way, the record says, Han came to know the kingdom of Dian while looking for a road to Daxia. The Tongjian, following the same account, keeps the arrival at Dian: the king of Dian asked the Han envoys which was the greater, Han or his own country — and the lord of Yelang asked the same question.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 19
11 / 18 In the sources
The second mission is the most extravagantly documented embassy of the age: a colonel of the palace guards with three hundred men, two horses apiece, cattle and sheep beyond counting, gold and silk goods worth tens of millions in cash, and a crowd of subordinate envoys each carrying a credential, to be detached to whatever country they passed. The memoir dates it by sequence and not by year: it follows the Xiongnu loss of the Hexi corridor in 121 and the battle north of the steppe in 119, which is why the mission is put at 119 BCE. The Wusun king received him on the model of the Chanyu's court, which is why the exchange over the gifts quoted above happens. It changed nothing else: the kingdom was divided in three among an old king, a rival prince with ten thousand horse and a grandson with another ten thousand, and the ministers, who had lived under the Xiongnu and feared them, would not hear of moving. He got no decision.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61; Hanshu, juan 96b
12 / 18 In the sources
The record of the end is one line — he was made Director of Ceremonies, ranked among the nine ministers, and died a year or so later. Then comes the verdict the episode is named for. Sima Qian's judgement of the man is a single image in two characters, bored a hole through — the sense is of driving a tunnel through rock — and the sentence that follows it says that after Zhang Qian, every envoy going west went under the name of the Marquis of Bowang to get standing among the foreign states, and that it was by that name they were believed. The Wusun's own first return party came with a handful of envoys and some tens of horses to see how big Han was, and went home reporting crowds and wealth; the treatise notes that the country then set more store by Han than before.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61
13 / 18 In the sources
The statistics are in both histories and are the nearest thing the sources have to a ledger of the road: an embassy of the great days was several hundred men, the smallest still a hundred; in a year the court sent ten-odd dispatches or five or six; the goods each carried were modelled on what Zhang Qian had taken. The memoirs then state the friction without embarrassment: the envoys were poor men's sons who had pocketed their state-issue goods and wanted to sell them cheap abroad; the western countries grew sick of Han envoys whose stories varied from one man to the next, calculated that Han's troops could not arrive, and withheld their food to make the visitors suffer; Han envoys ran short, and quarrelled and came to blows among themselves. Loulan and Gushi, astride the empty stretch, robbed them outright and killed them, until a marquis with seven hundred light horse rode in and took Loulan's king. After that the beacon towers ran from Jiuquan to the Jade Gate.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61; Hanshu, juan 96a
14 / 18 In the sources
The description is in the Book of Han's Western Regions treatise, and it is an argument against going: when Chengdi's court wanted to send an envoy back to the kingdom of Jibin beyond the Suspended Crossing, Du Qin, adviser to the Grand General Wang Feng, read out what the road actually cost, and the Grand General reported his advice to the throne and it was taken. Out beyond Pishan lie the Greater and Lesser Headache Mountains, the Red Earth and the Fever Slope, where travellers turn bright red with heat or are sick with headache and the pack animals die of it; then the Three Pools and the Rock Slope, the track one chi six or seven cun wide running thirty li along the edge of a precipice, horsemen and porters clinging to one another and hauling each other with cords, two thousand li to the place called the Suspended Crossing — where a beast that falls is dashed to pieces before it reaches the bottom, and a man who falls cannot be recovered at all. His conclusion: envoys carrying the strong Han's credential sit hungry in mountain valleys with nothing to beg, and after ten or twenty days their men and animals are left in the open country and never come back; the wise king attends to what is inside the nine regions and does not go looking for what is outside. Let the mission turn back at Pishan. Du Qin got his way — and the treatise then adds the reason Jibin kept coming anyway: it wanted the rewards and the market, and its envoys came once every few years.
Hanshu, juan 96a, Treatise on the Western Regions
15 / 18 In the sources
The treatise says the city-states of the west, settled and walled and unlike the horse peoples, were all made to serve the Xiongnu, who taxed them and took what they needed, with a Commandant of Bond Servants set up by the Rizhu King of the Xiongnu's western border, the commandant himself lodged among Yanzhi, Weixu and Weili. The Han's counter-price is recorded in the same books and in the same plain words. When the Wusun asked for a Han marriage the court answered that the bride-price came first; the Wusun sent a thousand horses; and in the Yuanfeng years a daughter of the king of Jiangdu went out as a princess to be the Wusun ruler's Right Lady, while a Xiongnu woman was made his Left Lady. She built her own quarters, saw the old king once or twice a year, could not speak with him, and wrote a song: my family has given me away to the edge of the sky, lodged me with the king of the Wusun; felt is my house and the felt is my wall, flesh my food and mare's milk my drink; I ache for my old land and wish I were a yellow swan going home. The emperor pitied her and sent her a present every other year. When the king grew old he meant her to pass to his grandson; she objected, and the emperor's answer was: follow the custom of her new country, since we intend to use the Wusun to destroy the Hu.
Hanshu, juan 96a and 96b; Shiji, juan 123
16 / 18 In the sources
The reasoning is quoted in both histories: Han is far from us, and in the salt marsh their parties keep coming to grief; to the north of it are the Hu, to the south no water or grass; the towns give out and there is nothing to eat, and when Han envoys come in parties of several hundred they are always short of food and more than half of them die. How could they bring a large army? They can do us nothing. And the horses of the secondary city are Ferghana's treasure. A Han envoy, angry at the refusal, cursed the place and destroyed the golden horse the court had carried out for the purpose, and left. A Ferghana noble said the envoy had treated them with contempt; and the king of the eastern border town was sent to intercept him, kill him and take what he had. That is what started the war.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61
17 / 18 In the sources
The emperor's motive is not hidden in the record: among the reasons he made Li Guangli a general was that he wanted to ennoble his favourite concubine's family. The first campaign reached the western town of Yucheng with a few thousand starving men, was beaten there, and got back to Dunhuang with one or two in ten; hence the order at the Jade Gate. The second took sixty thousand men out of the garrison commanderies, with the convicts of seven categories of penalty, oxen by the hundred thousand, horses and donkeys and camels to match, and transport parties reaching in from the east; a further 180,000 garrison troops were posted north of Jiuquan and Zhangye to hold the line. Ferghana's outer town fell, water was diverted from under the walls, and the nobles killed King Wu Ga and sent his head out with the horses — the Han chose some tens of the best and drove off three thousand-odd of the middling stock, and of the army that came back through the Jade Gate there were a little over ten thousand men and a thousand horses. It took four years, and it is what made the western states come in.
Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61
18 / 18 Commentary
The Book of the Later Han states the two facts that the phrase about an open road tends to cover. Usurping Wang Mang made enemies of the western states; they broke with China and went back under the Xiongnu, and when the dynasty came back to the question the treatise dates the gap at sixty-five years. The road was picked up again in 73 CE and a Protector-General was first posted again the next year, but that officer was overwhelmed when Emperor Ming died and the post was left vacant under Emperor Zhang until 91 CE, when Ban Chao had settled the western states and was made Protector-General himself, and fifty-odd countries then sent sons in as hostages; later, after Emperor He died, the region revolted, the court judged the place too far and too dangerous to relieve, abolished the post of Protector-General, and from then the Western Regions were abandoned — with men at court urging the closure of the Jade Gate and the Yang Gate outright. Its other honest note is about the west's own account of itself: the envoys of Daqin, the land beyond the western sea, arrived at the court in 166 CE by way of the southern commandery of Rinan, claiming to come from a king called Andun; and the book adds that the tribute they brought had nothing rare about it, and that the ones who had carried the story had probably exaggerated.
Hou Hanshu, juan 88, Memoir of the Western Regions; Hanshu, juan 96a

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Zhang Qian opened the Silk Road: a trade route in which Chinese silk crossed Asia to be sold in Rome.

What the sources say

The name is not ancient — Seidenstraße was coined by the geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877. What the histories call it is zao kong — boring a hole through — and the traffic they describe is state traffic: envoys carrying gold and silk goods worth tens of millions in cash as gifts and bribes, to buy alliances against the Xiongnu. Silk did move west — the Han envoys are caught privately selling the state's goods, and the Parthians are recorded buying and re-exporting — but the mission's purpose was encirclement, not commerce, and Rome is not in any of its reports.

Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61 and 96a; the name is modern
The story you may know

Zhang Qian brought the grape, the walnut, the sesame, garlic and the pomegranate into China.

What the sources say

The memoir names two plants coming back out of the west and does not attach them to Zhang Qian personally: grape and alfalfa, whose seed Han envoys collected, and which were then planted on the good ground around the imperial parks until they covered the view. The walnut, the sesame and the pomegranate are added by later books, which like to give an import a name and a date. Sesame in particular is argued from modern finds to have been grown in China well before the mission.

Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 96a; the wider list is a later accretion
The story you may know

Zhang Qian, or one of his envoys, reached Daqin — the Roman empire — and opened direct relations with it.

What the sources say

The farthest named destinations in the reports are Tiaozhi and the western sea, and they are second-hand: the Book of Han puts Tiaozhi a hundred-odd days beyond the last state the Han reached, and says that going west by water from there you get somewhere near the setting of the sun. The Book of the Later Han states it plainly as a correction: earlier Han envoys all turned back at Wuyi and not one of them reached Tiaozhi. In 97 CE an officer of Ban Chao got to the shore and gave up when Parthian sailors told him the crossing could take three months with a fair wind or two years without, and that men died of longing on it.

Hanshu, juan 96a; Hou Hanshu, juan 88, Memoir of the Western Regions
The story you may know

Once Zhang Qian opened it, the Silk Road stayed open for the next thousand years.

What the sources say

The records date the closures as carefully as the openings. Wang Mang's handling of the western states broke the relation, the Xiongnu took the taxing-post back, and the region was severed from China for the sixty-five years before Ban Chao. The post of Protector-General was then abolished a second time under Emperor An, and men at court proposed shutting the Jade Gate and the Yang Gate. What the Han built in the west was a garrison system — four commanderies, two passes, beacon towers, and farmer-soldiers at Luntai and Queli — and it ran only as long as the treasury paid for it.

Hanshu, juan 96a; Hou Hanshu, juan 88
The story you may know

Zhang Qian spent thirteen years a prisoner in chains in the Xiongnu camp.

What the sources say

Thirteen years is the length of the absence, not of the captivity, and the record says he was not confined: he was held ten-odd years, given a wife, had a son, and grew familiar enough with his captors to slip away — and was taken a second time, for about a year, on the way home along the southern edge of the plateau. The two details that survive from the decade are the one the historian chose to keep: he never let go of the Han credential, and the staff he carried had an empire's use for it later.

Shiji, juan 123; Hanshu, juan 61; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 18
