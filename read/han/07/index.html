Ashes in Chang'an

The Palace Digging and the Luntai Edict, 91–89 BCE — Eight years after five thousand Han foot were left without bread, the city that bled for them began digging up its own palaces for buried figures, and the search ran from a chancellor's cell to the empress's chambers to the eastern palace, until the heir it was all wasted on hanged himself two hundred li south.

01 / 18 Note
Eight chapters of the Book of the Han carry this episode: juan 6 for the dates; juan 63 for the crown prince's house; juan 45 for the man who made the searching an occupation; juan 66 for two chancellors and for the officer who spoke for the prince at the end; juan 55 and juan 97 upper for the empress's family; juan 8 and juan 74 for the child left behind; juan 96 lower for the edict that stopped the war. Ban Gu files the prince under his posthumous name, as the memoir of the Li park. The Zizhi tongjian is used as a relay and not as a witness, and a detail existing only there is marked. The court story-books are used only to be rebutted.
On our sources
02 / 18 In the sources
The order of the entries is the argument against the picture of a mob: a levy of knights, a sealed capital, eleven days, a prohibition two years old. The chancellor was Gongsun He, an old cavalry officer of the Wei house's wars, who wept when the seal went into his hands and said he was not equal to it. His son Jing Sheng, the empress's sister's son, had used nineteen million cash of the Northern Army's money; the chancellor caught a great knight of Yangling named Zhu Anshi to buy his son off, and heard back from prison that the bamboo of the southern mountains was not enough for his words. Father and son died in prison in the first month. Ban Gu's summary: the calamity rose from Zhu Anshi, was completed by Jiang Chong, and reached the princesses, the empress and the crown prince.
Hanshu, juan 6, Annals of Emperor Wu; juan 66, Biographies of Gongsun, Liu, Tian, Wang, Yang, Cai, Chen and Zheng
03 / 18 In the sources
The book keeps the buried figures and the adultery in one sentence, which is why the case could not be answered: a man could disprove a figure in the ground and could not disprove a bed. The year-book enters the princesses under the intercalary seventh month, and nothing of theirs under the fifth. Ban Gu's digger is a nameless shaman of the northern peoples; a name appears only in the mirror, and the name this affair carries in modern retelling is in no chapter here. His end is one clause in the prince's biography: when the prince's men held the city, the shaman was roasted alive in the imperial park.
Hanshu, juan 6; juan 63, Biographies of the Five Sons of Emperor Wu
04 / 18 In the sources
Carriages were confiscated and the sons of the great ordered in to serve with the Northern Army until their families bought them off, in sums the book puts at tens of millions of cash. He stopped the emperor's aunt driving on the carriageway reserved for the emperor; when she pleaded an order from the dowager empress, he answered that the princess might ride and her escort might not, and reported the whole train. The emperor's verdict on him is four words: loyal, upright, law-bound, unafraid. The chapter's closing comment quotes Confucius hating the glib mouth that overturns a house, then runs through a list of men who destroyed those closest to them — his pair is the poison wood and the crown prince — and draws the lesson that the small overturned the great and the distant trapped the kin. The emperor, it says elsewhere, learned later that Jiang Chong had been lying, and wiped out three agnate groups of his kindred. His last title matters most because it is no office at all: commissioner of the witchcraft cases, with nothing behind him but the emperor's fear.
Hanshu, juan 45, Biographies of Kuai Tong, Wu Bei, Jiang Chong and Xi Fugong; juan 63
05 / 18 In the sources
Ban Gu puts the motive in front of the words: he saw the emperor to be old and feared the crown prince after him, and so made his treachery by reporting that the sickness came from witchcraft. The fuller form of the report, and the shaman's name, are in the mirror. What follows in both books is procedure and not mania: a commission, a specialist, a search, torture licensed to produce what the search had found, and a capital charge that drew a man's family in with him. For numbers keep Ban Gu's own phrase — tens of thousands of persons dead, first and last — and not the larger totals later retelling gives it.
Hanshu, juan 45; Zizhi tongjian, scroll 22
06 / 18 In the sources
The informer's chapter gives the order of the searching and the mirror follows it — first the concubines who hoped for visits, then in order up to the empress, then the crown prince — and that order is the whole case against reading it as a crowd: hope made the first list and rank made the sequence. The prince's chapter supplies the harder detail, that the emperor's seat in his own chambers was taken down to open the floor. The paulownia figures are Ban Gu's; the rolls of silk with a writing that rebelled against the Way, and the line that the trenching left the prince and the empress no place to set a bed, are the mirror's. The year-book, which contains itself entirely, enters the seventh month of 91 BCE with the marquis, the censor and the commissioner digging in the palace of the crown prince, and adds nothing.
Hanshu, juan 63; juan 45; juan 6; Zizhi tongjian, scroll 22
07 / 18 In the sources
That letter stands in the chancellor's biography, where the new minister was trying to persuade the emperor to ease his mind, and it damages the proceedings more than anything written against them afterwards: the search began with the women of the Ganquan Palace, moved to the pepper-chambers of the Weiyang, took in the chancellor's son's crowd, and never came before a court. The emperor adds that he ate one meal a day for months and could not bear the sound of music. No chapter gives a list of the accused, or proof that one figure was buried with the intent charged; and the rule often quoted, that a convicted man not killed to the end was thereby disloyal and obscene, is not in these chapters and is not used here.
Hanshu, juan 66; juan 45; juan 63
08 / 18 In the sources
The general's biography, which is where a search of his family's graves would have to appear, says nothing of any digging. It records that he married the emperor's sister the Princess of Pingyang, that he lies with her, and that his mound was raised in the shape of Mount Lu; for the end of his house, one sentence — the Wei were destroyed when the prince failed. The book says the empress bore three daughters before it says she bore a son, and names two princesses executed on this charge; the children killed at one time in the prince's chapter are his sons and daughter, not hers. That the general had been the emperor's servant before he was his brother-in-law is why this fall is a matter of procedure and not of jealousy: the court did not have to open graves to finish a family.
Hanshu, juan 55, Biographies of Wei Qing and Huo Qubing; juan 63; juan 97 upper, Biographies of the Outer Kin
09 / 18 In the sources
Ban Gu's entry for her is the plainest thing in the book: thirty-eight years as empress, a beginning as a singer, the favour fading, the witchcraft breaking out and Jiang Chong using it, the prince frightened and unable to make himself clear, and so the empress and her son killed Chong and he raised troops and lost. The year-book is flatter: on the renwu day the prince and the empress planned the killing of Chong; on the gengyin day the prince fled and the empress killed herself. She was laid in a small coffin in an empty storeroom of the Public Coach and buried at Tongbai outside the southern wall; her entry names the two inner attendants who carried her out, and one of them is the man who had been sent to help with the digging. The house of Wei was destroyed entire. Her great-grandson, coming out of a gaol to the throne, buried her again with a posthumous name and a park of three hundred households.
Hanshu, juan 97 upper; juan 6; juan 63
10 / 18 Debated
The speech, the precedent — Fusu, the First Emperor's eldest son, who took the sword when the order came — and the prince's agreement are all Ban Gu's, and so is the sentence that he was frightened and could not make himself clear. Not in Ban Gu is the other answer: the mirror makes the prince say that he is a man's son and cannot execute a minister on his own authority, that he would rather go in and apologise, and that he started for the summer palace and was pressed too hard to reach it. The relay gives a man trying to surrender; the history gives a man choosing an act. Both give his reason for hurry: the emperor believed everyone at his elbow was cursing him, and no one dared plead for another man.
Hanshu, juan 63; Zizhi tongjian, scroll 22
11 / 18 In the sources
The curse stands in the informer's chapter as well as in the prince's, and in both it is the prince's own definition of the crime: not sorcery but the making of enmity between a father and a son, and the insult is a fact about the man, who was born in Handan. The censor got out with a wound and reached the summer palace, and so did the inner attendant who had been sent to help with the digging; it is the attendant who, in the mirror's account, had informed on the prince to his father years before and who now spoke against him to the emperor, and he was burnt on the Heng bridge when the accounts were settled. Ban Gu gives the killing; the mirror adds the parading of the body. Neither book pretends that any of the three died under sentence.
Hanshu, juan 45; juan 63; juan 6; juan 66
12 / 18 In the sources
The five days and the tens of thousands are in the chancellor's biography and in the year-book; the prince's chapter gives the other reason the week was lost — the capital was confused and it was given out that the prince had rebelled, so the crowd would not hold with him. The empire's two answers were permanent. The Han staff was plain red, and because a crown prince had carried a red one a yellow yak-tail was added above the red; and the year-book enters that change, beside the first standing garrison of the gates of Chang'an, among the acts of state of the month. The settlement ran by category: the prince's guests were executed, the men who had raised troops with him were exterminated under the law for rebellion, and the soldiers who had plundered were sent to Dunhuang.
Hanshu, juan 66; juan 6; juan 63
13 / 18 In the sources
The year-book puts the flight on the gengyin day of the seventh month and the death on the xinhai day of the eighth, and the chancellor's biography says the prince was taken twenty-odd days after he left the city, which is what those two day-names come to; an earthquake follows in the annal within twelve days. Two men broke in, one kicking the door open and one running to lift the body down, and were made marquises under a decree that states the reason: a reward given where the merit is doubtful is how a government makes its credit good. The biography adds that the officers who had used blades on the prince were afterwards wiped out — and that the emperor, pitying the prince's innocence, built the Palace of Thinking of Him and raised at Hu the Terrace of Returning and Looking. No chapter says the town was renamed.
Hanshu, juan 6; juan 63; juan 66
14 / 18 In the sources
The officer was Bing Ji, who had begun as a jail clerk in Lu state reading out the statutes; the annal of the child's own reign writes his surname with a different character, which is the kind of thing two chapters of one book do. He held the gate from midnight until sunrise and was impeached for it, and the sentence that ends the affair is the emperor's: he too came to himself, said that this was the work of a spirit, and amnestied the empire. The boy went back to the household of his great-grandmother the Lady Shi, who had died with the prince. The memorial that brought him to the throne in 74 BCE gives his age as eighteen.
Hanshu, juan 74, Biographies of Wei Xiang and Bing Ji; juan 8, Annals of Emperor Xuan; juan 63
15 / 18 In the sources
The emperor's letter to Quli during the street war has the Duke of Zhou in it — the business was buzzing all over the empire, what did he mean by calling it a secret, and did not the Duke of Zhou execute his own brothers — which makes being the emperor's nephew no defence. Three officers died over the prince's escape: his aide Tian Ren, who let him through a gate at night; the Imperial Counsellor Bao Shengzhi, driven to suicide for stopping that execution, since an officer of two thousand shi must be reported before he is killed; and the commander of the Northern Army's guard, Ren An, who took the prince's staff, went in, shut his gate, and was executed for meaning to join whichever side won. Li Guangli's offer to the chancellor, at the Wei bridge, had been to make the King of Changyi heir. Ren An is the man the historian's great letter addresses.
Hanshu, juan 66; juan 6; juan 63
16 / 18 In the sources
The emperor's answer is on the record: between a father and a son other men cannot speak, and it is the spirit of the High Ancestor who sent you to teach me. The elder of Huguan had put it plainer — a man of no family was given a command to press the heir, the roads between father and son were blocked, and the young man only stole his father's weapons to get out of a difficulty, and the elder did not believe his heart was wicked. Ban Gu's entry on that letter is that the emperor was moved and woke, and still did not say openly that the prince had been innocent. The man of the dream is written Che in the prince's chapter and Tian in the chancellor's, from the old royal house of Qi; he was called the Carriage Chancellor because his years let him bring a small cart into the palace. The book says he had no other talent, and that it had never happened before that a man took a chancellorship in a month on one sentence.
Hanshu, juan 63; juan 66; Zizhi tongjian, scroll 22
17 / 18 Debated
The request is in the western regions memoir, and it is the centre's paperwork rather than a frontier report: it comes from the grain commandant with the two ministers, it asks to extend garrison-land already held at Luntai, Jiezhi and Quli, and it wants volunteers with families and goods behind them, not convicts. The same memoir gives Ban Gu's frame: the first opening of the west, then armies going out continuously, the realm emptied and wasted, a whole command lost to the enemy, and the emperor sorry for the distant wars. The year-book for 89 BCE enters the emperor ploughing at Juding with his own hand and renewing the seal-mound of Mount Tai, and says nothing about Luntai; the mirror does the dating. The officer who signed this request sat eight years later in the minister's chairs defending that frontier against the provincial appeals men, and was executed in 80 BCE in a plot against the regent.
Hanshu, juan 96 lower, Memoir of the Western Regions; juan 6; juan 66; Zizhi tongjian, scroll 22
18 / 18 Commentary
Ban Gu's phrase for what this document does is that the emperor set out at length his regret for what was past; the edict itself is administrative rather than penitential, and the book gives it no special name. The grander confession — that since his accession everything he had done had been mad, and that there are no immortals — is in the mirror, spoken after a wind drove him back from the sea where he meant to sail for the gods, and is not in the histories. Ban Gu's own summary of these years, closing the prince's chapter, is the darkest sentence written about them: the capital bleeding, corpses in the tens of thousands, prince, son and father all destroyed; and soldiers are like fire, and unless you put them out you are burnt in your own burning. Sima Guang rules that this emperor's faults were the Qin's faults and that he escaped the Qin's end for one reason: he could regret, and at the last he chose trustees who held. He died at the Wuzuo Palace in the second month of 87 BCE, two days after naming his youngest son heir. The great-grandson took the throne in 74 BCE, gave the prince the severe posthumous name li and two hundred households at his park at Hu, gave the prince's consort thirty grave-keepers and the boy's own parents a park of three hundred households, since the ritual forbade an emperor to sacrifice to the father through whom he had not inherited, and eight years later raised that park to a temple and a county of sixteen hundred households.
Hanshu, juan 96 lower; juan 63; juan 6; juan 8; Zizhi tongjian, scrolls 22 and 29

Myth Check

The Ming romances of the Han dynasties, the stage plays drawn from them and the serials drawn from those are entertainment — and not a source. Where their famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

The witchcraft cases of 91 BCE were a panic: a superstitious old emperor frightened of sorcery, and conjurers and women running wild through the capital.

What the sources say

It was a legal machine and every part of it is named: a commission, a specialist who read the ground, assistants drawn from the imperial staff, torture licensed to produce confessions, and a standing capital charge that brought in a man's family with him. The search ran in a stated order — the concubines who hoped for the emperor's visits, then the empress's chambers, then the eastern palace, with the emperor's own seat lifted off its floor. The emperor admitted afterwards, in a letter kept in a chancellor's biography, that neither the Nine Ministers nor the Commandant of Justice had ever properly tried anyone. Fear supplied the accusations; the procedure supplied the deaths.

Hanshu, juan 45; juan 63; juan 6; juan 66
The story you may know

Emperor Wu killed his own son.

What the sources say

The son killed himself: he barred the door of the inner room at the storehouse in Hu and hanged when the officers had the place surrounded. His father wrote the sealed letter that told his chancellor to fight him in the streets with orders to kill and wound in numbers, which is the most damning thing in the episode and not the killing; and he afterwards rewarded the two men who broke in and then destroyed them, wiped out the Jiang kindred, had the eunuch who worked with Jiang Chong burnt on a bridge, and built a palace and a terrace at Hu for thinking of him. The dynasty never treated the prince as a criminal: his grandson came out of a gaol, took the throne, and rebuilt and endowed his tomb.

Hanshu, juan 63; juan 6; juan 66; juan 8
The story you may know

The Luntai edict of 89 BCE is where expansion ended: the emperor repented, the west was given up, and the empire turned inward.

What the sources say

The edict refused one proposal — a fresh extension of garrison-farming and beacon-towers further west, on a road the emperor said had starved thousands on the march home even after a victory — and it refused it in fiscal language, with instructions about exactions, farming and the horse-corvee. Nothing already held was given up: Luntai was farmed again within a few years under the officer who had asked for it, and the protectorate over the western regions was established in 60 BCE and held for the rest of the dynasty. Ban Gu's one-line summary, that from that time no army went out again, is about the emperor's remaining years, not about the empire's map.

Hanshu, juan 96 lower, Memoir of the Western Regions; juan 70; Discourses on Salt and Iron
The story you may know

The fall of the Wei house is a harem story: a neglected empress, a witch in the women's apartments, and the great general's graves dug up out of spite.

What the sources say

The grave-digging is not in the histories. The general's own biography, which records his marriage to the emperor's sister and the shape of his mound, says only that the five Wei marquisates were stripped within twenty-four years and that the house was destroyed when the crown prince failed; the searching did reach the empress's quarters and her nephew died under the charge, which was enough. Her own entry names no witch at all: thirty-eight years as empress, the rise of rival consorts, the commission, the demand for her seal, her suicide and a small coffin in a storeroom. The spirits and the love that curdled come from the palace story-books, Miscellaneous Records of the Western Capital and Stories of Emperor Wu of Han, which are much later than Ban Gu.

Hanshu, juan 55; juan 97 upper. Against the wonders: Xijing zaji and Han wu gushi
The story you may know

The speeches and wonders in the mirror of history — the emperor's dream of wooden men, the prince's wish to go and explain himself, the confession by the sea — are independent traditions filling gaps the Book of the Han left.

What the sources say

They are Sima Guang's workshop, cutting these same chapters into a dated line eleven centuries later and here and there taking in a detail Ban Gu left out or never carried. That is why those three items, and the search that sweeps from the capital commanderies across the whole empire, are marked in the notes as the relay's and are never used as fact. The rule runs the other way too: where the mirror and Ban Gu differ, it is usually Ban Gu's own chapters differing with each other — over whether the years of marching are thirty or thirty-two, and over whether the emperor's death and his heir's appointment fall a day apart or two.

Zizhi tongjian, scroll 22; Hanshu, juan 63; juan 6; juan 96 lower; juan 7, Annals of Emperor Zhao
