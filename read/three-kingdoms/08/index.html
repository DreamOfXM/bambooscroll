High Water

Fancheng and Jing Province, 219 CE — In the autumn of 219 the rains gave Guan Yu the greatest victory of his life — and within months the same river carried away his army, his province, and his head.

01 / 17 Note
Episode 8 is told, like the others, only from the official histories: the Records of the Three Kingdoms, Pei Songzhi's fifth-century commentary, and the Zizhi Tongjian. The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is used nowhere as a source; where it disagrees, the Myth Check at the end says so.
On our sources
02 / 17 In the sources
The biography sets the campaign immediately after Liu Bei's taking of Hanzhong and his kingship. What the record does not say is whether Guan Yu moved on orders from Chengdu or on his own authority; that silence has been argued over ever since.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6, Biography of Guan Yu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
03 / 17 In the sources
Pang De's vow is in his own biography, and so are the doubts of the other generals, whose cause was that his elder brother served Liu Bei in Shu. Cao Cao dismissed the doubts and gave Pang De his place in the van; the biography adds that Cao Ren set his camp on the open ground ten li north of Fancheng.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 18, Biography of Pang De; Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Ren; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
04 / 17 In the sources
The failed supplies and the threat — “when he returned he would deal with them” (還當治之) — are in Guan Yu's biography, where they stand immediately before the story of the two officers' surrender. Mi Fang was Liu Bei's own kinsman by marriage; that neither rank nor kinship survived his fear says what the fear was worth.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6, Biography of Guan Yu
05 / 17 In the sources
The flood is recorded as weather, not contrivance: “it chanced that the heavens rained greatly” (會天大雨), and Yu Jin's biography says nothing of dams or diverted water. Guan Yu's advantage was that he had ships and the camps had none.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Yu Jin; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
06 / 17 In the sources
The surrender is in Yu Jin's biography: on the embankment, with no road left, he gave himself up; Guan Yu took him south and held him under arrest at Jiangling. “Flooding the seven armies” (水淹七軍) is a fair summary of the record — of a natural flood exploited, not made.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Yu Jin; Wei shu 18, Biography of Pang De
07 / 17 In the sources
The refusal to kneel and the death are in Pang De's biography; the mourning and the posthumous honours are in the same scroll. The histories record no terms offered and none accepted — only a prisoner who stood, cursed, and died, and a court that mourned him as proof of what loyalty was.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 18, Biography of Pang De; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
08 / 17 In the sources
The flood reached Fancheng too, and Man Chong's biography preserves the answer given to those who urged abandonment: the flood was swift and would not last, and to quit Fancheng was to quit everything south of the Yellow River. Xu Huang's relief army was already on the road.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 26, Biography of Man Chong; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
09 / 17 In the sources
The phrase and the proposal to move the capital are both in Guan Yu's biography. Sima Yi and Jiang Ji answered with the plan that ended the campaign: Sun Quan would not stomach Guan Yu's greatness — promise him the south of the river, and the siege lifts itself. Cao Cao took the advice.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6, Biography of Guan Yu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
10 / 17 Debated
The pact is in every account; its origin is not the same account. The Wei side traces it to Cao Cao's court and a promised cession of the lands south of the river; the Wu side gives Sun Quan his own motives, with the insulted marriage envoy as the spark. Both had reason; the records disagree on who moved first.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6; Wei shu 1; Wu shu 9; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
11 / 17 In the sources
The feigned illness and the substitution are in Lu Meng's biography: the sickness was stratagem, and it was Lu Meng who named Lu Xun as his successor — his thoughts run deep, he said, and his plans are equal to the weight. The withdrawal of Guan Yu's rearward troops is in Guan Yu's biography.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 9, Biography of Lu Meng; Shu shu 6, Biography of Guan Yu
12 / 17 In the sources
The letter is paraphrased from Lu Xun's biography, which quotes it at length: the praise of the victory over Yu Jin, the self-deprecation of a scholar, and the deference that did the work. Guan Yu read the letter, was greatly pleased, and moved more troops to the front. The flattery was policy; the caution it disarmed was real.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 13, Biography of Lu Xun; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
13 / 17 In the sources
The white-clad crossing (白衣渡江) is in Lu Meng's biography: the troops hidden in the hulls, the rowers in plain white dress (白衣) in the guise of merchants, moving day and night, and the riverside watch-posts taken one by one so that no word got through. White here is the plain dress of commoners, not mourning.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 9, Biography of Lu Meng; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
14 / 17 In the sources
The surrenders and the discipline are in Lu Meng's biography: the households of Guan Yu's soldiers were treated with studied kindness, and the treasuries sealed to await Sun Quan. None of it was mercy alone; every act of it was aimed at the army still sitting before Fancheng.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 9, Biography of Lu Meng; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
15 / 17 In the sources
The unravelling is in Lu Meng's biography: the messengers well used, the soldiers asking secretly after their households, and no man with the will to fight. Xu Huang's counter-stroke before Fancheng broke the siege from without at the same time; the desertion broke it from within.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 9, Biography of Lu Meng; Wei shu 17, Biography of Xu Huang
16 / 17 Debated
Shu shu 6 places the death at Linju; the Wu accounts and the Zizhi Tongjian have the capture at Zhangxiang after the flight from Maicheng, and the false surrender with it. The two versions stand close enough to argue over and far enough apart to matter; the sources were not reconciled. Sun Quan sent the head to Cao Cao.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6, Biography of Guan Yu; Wu shu 10, Biography of Pan Zhang; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
17 / 17 Commentary
Chen Shou's appraisal grants the general his due — a tiger-minister of the age, a match for ten thousand — and then names the flaw: fierce, and self-regarding (剛而自矜); his defeat came by his shortcoming, and that, it says, is the usual reckoning. The victor's reckoning was shorter: Lu Meng died within the season, aged forty-two.
Chen Shou's appraisal, Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6; Wu shu 9; Wei shu 17

Myth Check

The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a masterpiece — and not a source. Where its famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Guan Yu released Cao Cao at the Huarong Trail, repaying an old kindness.

What the sources say

The scene is the novel's, and it is in the wrong war: Huarong belongs to the retreat from Red Cliff in 208, eleven years before Fancheng, and Guan Yu is nowhere in the record of it. What the sources give is that Liu Bei reached the trail after Cao Cao had passed, tried to pursue, and missed him; Cao Cao himself is quoted laughing that Liu Bei was his equal but slow to plan.

Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 50; Shanyang Gong Zaiji, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Wei shu 1
The story you may know

The single-blade meeting was Guan Yu's feat: he went alone into Wu's camp and overawed them.

What the sources say

The meeting happened in 215, over the commanderies of southern Jing, and the bold one in the histories is Lu Su. It was Lu Su who invited the parley with only the two commanders carrying single blades, and Lu Su who dressed Guan Yu down over the disputed commanderies while the armies sat a hundred paces off. The novel turned the guest into the host of the courage.

Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 66; Sanguozhi, Wu shu 10, Biography of Lu Su
The story you may know

Guan Yu loosed the waters himself and drowned the seven armies by design.

What the sources say

The flood is in the record as weather: autumn rains, the Han River over its banks, fathoms of water on the level ground — and Yu Jin's biography says not a word of dams or diversions. What was Guan Yu's was the exploitation: he kept ships, the camps had none, and he attacked the drowned army from the water. The skill needs no sorcery; the record gives it better.

Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 74; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Yu Jin
The story you may know

Hua Tuo scraped the poison from Guan Yu's bone while he played chess.

What the sources say

The bone-scraping is real and in Guan Yu's biography: an arrow wound gone to the bone, an arm cut open, the poison scraped out, and the patient eating and drinking through it. But the physician in the sources is nameless, and Hua Tuo was already dead — killed by Cao Cao years before this campaign. The game of chess is the novel's addition to a scene that needed none.

Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 75; Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6, Biography of Guan Yu; Wei shu 29, Biography of Hua Tuo
