The Gates at Dawn

Luoyang, 249 CE — The emperor rode out to pray at the tombs; behind him an old man who had been dying for two years closed the gates of the capital — by nightfall the regent had surrendered, and within ten days he was dead.

Episode 10 is told, like the others, only from the official histories: the Records of the Three Kingdoms with Pei Songzhi's fifth-century commentary, the Annals of Emperor Xuan in the Book of Jin, and the Zizhi Tongjian. On our sources
02 / 16 In the sources
The arrangement is in the annals of the young emperor and in Cao Shuang's biography: Cao Fang came to the throne a child, Cao Shuang and Sima Yi were both charged with the orphan-king, and Sima Yi was soon translated to the office of Grand Tutor — a promotion that took him out of command. The demotion, not the illness, is where this story starts.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 4, Annals of the Three Young Emperors; Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Shuang; Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan
03 / 16 Commentary
The charges come from the Wei shu preserved in Pei Songzhi's commentary and written after Sima Yi's victory: Cao Shuang's meals and dress patterned on the imperial style, the late emperor's talented women kept in his own quarters. The victors kept the books on the fallen; this episode presents them as the record's, which is to say the victors', account.
Wei shu, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9
04 / 16 Debated
The performance for Li Sheng is recorded both in the Jin shu annals and in the Wei shu preserved by Pei Songzhi, and this episode stages it as the sources tell it. Whether it was pure acting is another matter: Sima Yi was seventy, had been genuinely ill before, and died within two years of the coup. The sources present stratagem because the victors wrote the memory; historians still argue the point.
Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Wei shu, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9
05 / 16 In the sources
The tomb visit is dated in the annals: first month of Zhengshi 10, day jiawu — 5 February 249 by the modern calendar. The Zizhi Tongjian notes that Cao Shuang and his brothers all rode in the procession; that was the morning Sima Yi had been waiting for since 247.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 4, Annals of the Three Young Emperors; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
06 / 16 In the sources
The retainers are the record's own detail: the Jin shu annals of Emperor Jing say Sima Shi had secretly gathered three thousand bold retainers willing to die for him, and that on the morning of the coup he led them to join his father. The coup was a family's work before it was one man's.
Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Jing; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
07 / 16 In the sources
The memorial survives in summary in the Jin shu annals — Cao Shuang charged with corrupting the young emperor's trust and filling the court with his own men — and the annals of the young emperor record the result in one line: the Grand Tutor Sima Yi memorialised the Empress Dowager to dismiss Cao Shuang. The coup began with a document.
Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 4, Annals of the Three Young Emperors
08 / 16 In the sources
Both accounts give the same bloodless shape: gates shut, armouries taken, troops sent to the Luo river floating bridge. No engagement is recorded anywhere in the coup itself. What happened at Gaoping was decided by whoever owned the city the tombs stood outside.
Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Shuang; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
09 / 16 In the sources
The promise is recorded twice: in the Jin shu annals, and in the Wei shu preserved by Pei Songzhi, which adds that Sima Yi swore by the river Luo. The oath mattered because men like Jiang Ji carried it; it mattered more because within ten days it was broken.
Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Wei shu, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9
10 / 16 In the sources
Huan Fan's flight with the seal and his advice to go to Xuchang are in Cao Shuang's biography and in the Zizhi Tongjian. The plan was workable and was refused; the record keeps it to show what the regent declined.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Shuang; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
11 / 16 Commentary
Both sayings come from the Wei shu preserved by Pei Songzhi — Cao Shuang comforting himself with a marquisate, and Huan Fan's weeping verdict on the sons of Cao Zidan. They are quotations kept by the victors because they explain the outcome.
Wei shu, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9
12 / 16 In the sources
The surrender is recorded step by step: the seal and ribbons sent up, the retainers dismissed, Cao Shuang returning to his mansion in the capital, where soldiers were at once posted around its walls. The Zizhi Tongjian gives the sequence without ornament.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Shuang; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
13 / 16 In the sources
The executions are in every account: the charge of great treason, the case handed to the law officers, and the extermination of the three branches of kin. The Zizhi Tongjian notes that the capital was shaken; the Jin shu annals record the fact and move on.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Shuang; Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
14 / 16 In the sources
Jiang Ji's biography keeps the ending: he and Chen Tai had gone to persuade Cao Shuang, pledging that he would lose office only; after the executions Jiang Ji died within the year, and the record says of remorse. The oath-keeper's death was the coup's quietest casualty.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 15, Biography of Jiang Ji; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
15 / 16 In the sources
The settlement is in the annals: Cao Fang remained emperor, Sima Yi was offered the chancellorship and the Nine Bestowals and refused them, and the real authority of the state passed to his house. The Wei throne stood sixteen more years as an office the Sima family administered.
Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 4, Annals of the Three Young Emperors
16 / 16 In the sources
The dates stand in the Jin shu annals and the Wei annals: Sima Yi died in 251, Cao Fang was deposed in 254 and died in retirement in 274, and the abdication to Jin came in 265. What this episode describes is the hinge itself: the day the real power of Wei changed hands.
Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan, Emperor Jing, Emperor Wen; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 4

In this episode

The people who carried this year. The sources disagree about almost everything else.

Sima Yi司馬懿
179–251 CE
Regent of Wei, demoted to the empty seat of Grand Tutor. Feigned illness for two years, rose in a single morning, took the capital without a fight and broke his oath within ten days. Died two years later with his house ruling in his place.
Cao Shuang曹爽
d. 249 CE
Co-regent and kinsman of the imperial house. Held the court through his brothers and favourites, pushed Sima Yi aside, and rode out of the city on the morning that mattered. Surrendered on a promise of safety; executed with his faction ten days later.
Cao Fang曹芳
c. 231–274 CE
Emperor of Wei from the age of eight. Rode out to the Gaoping Tombs and rode back a guest in his own palace; deposed by the Sima house in 254, he died in retirement in 274.
Jiang Ji蔣濟
d. 249 CE
Veteran minister whose name gave weight to an oath. His letter to the camp promised Cao Shuang no more than the loss of office; he watched the promise break, and died of remorse within months.
Sima Shi司馬師
208–255 CE
Sima Yi's elder son. Quietly gathered three thousand sworn retainers who answered when the gates closed; after his father's death he ruled Wei in all but name.

Myth Check

The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a masterpiece — and not a source. Where its famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

The Gaoping Tombs was a battle — armies meeting outside Luoyang.

What the sources say

No engagement is recorded anywhere. The coup was won by paperwork and gates: an edict from the Empress Dowager, the city gates closed, the armouries seized, and an envoy's promise. The only violence came afterwards, at the executions. It was a seizure of the capital, not a battle in the field.

Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
The story you may know

Sima Yi swore Cao Shuang's safety by the river Luo — and kept his word.

What the sources say

The oath is recorded, and it was broken within ten days: Cao Shuang and his faction were charged with great treason and executed with all three branches of their kin. The promise was the instrument of the surrender, not its terms.

Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Shuang; Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
The story you may know

Sima Yi played the invalid for decades — the patient lifelong long-con of legend.

What the sources say

The record is narrower and stranger: he served as co-regent and commander from 239 to 247 — leading Wei's defence against Shu's invasion of 243–244 — then claimed illness from 247, staged it for Li Sheng in 248, and struck in 249. Two years of performance, not twenty; and since he died within two years of the coup, some of the illness may have been real. The sources want it read as pure cunning.

Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Xuan; Wei shu, in Pei Songzhi's commentary; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 4
The story you may know

The coup was one old man's solo stroke.

What the sources say

It was a family conspiracy with an official face: the Jin shu annals of Emperor Jing record Sima Shi's three thousand secretly gathered retainers, Sima Zhao was in the plot, the Empress Dowager's edict gave it legality, and ministers like Jiang Ji carried its promises to the camp.

Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Jing and Emperor Xuan; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 75
The story you may know

Wei fell that day — 249 ended the Cao dynasty.

What the sources say

Cao Fang remained emperor five more years and was deposed in 254; his successors reigned until 265, when Sima Yi's grandson took the throne by the abdication ritual and named the dynasty Jin. The coup did not end Wei; it made the ending certain.

Sanguozhi, Wei shu 4, Annals of the Three Young Emperors; Jin shu, Annals of Emperor Wu

  • Zizhi Tongjian — Sima Guang 1084 CE Scroll 75, Zhengshi 10 (249 CE): the coup told in sequence — the journey to the tombs, the memorial, the persuasion, the surrender, and the executions.
  • Wei shu (History of Wei) — various, 3rd c. CE 3rd c. CE Known now only in the fragments Pei Songzhi quoted: the fullest account of Cao Shuang's regency and its end, written by the side that won.
