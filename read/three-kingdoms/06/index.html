The City and the Ford

Hefei, 215 CE — Sun Quan brought a kingdom's strength against one northern city; a sealed letter, eight hundred picked men at dawn, and one broken bridge decided what the river kept.

Episode 6 is told, like the others, only from the official histories: the Records of the Three Kingdoms, Pei Songzhi's fifth-century commentary, and the Zizhi Tongjian. On our sources
02 / 16 In the sources
The sealed orders are in Zhang Liao's biography: Cao Cao was campaigning against Zhang Lu in Hanzhong, and left instructions with the protector Xue Ti, marked at the envelope's edge to be opened only when the enemy came. What the letter said, the garrison would not learn until Sun Quan was already before the walls.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 67
03 / 16 Debated
A hundred thousand is the strength the sources give for Sun Quan's army, and a later Wei edict repeats it when praising the defence. It is a claim, not a muster: round numbers of this size in the sources mark the size of the danger, not the size of the camp. This episode treats it so throughout.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao; Wu shu 2, Annals of Sun Quan
04 / 16 In the sources
The garrison's strength is given by the Zizhi Tongjian: Zhang Liao, Li Dian and Yue Jin with rather more than seven thousand men, stationed at Hefei while Cao Cao was in Hanzhong. The numbers were the reason the sealed letter existed; the feud between Zhang Liao and Li Dian was the reason it might not have been enough.
Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 67; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 18, Biography of Li Dian
05 / 16 In the sources
The letter's wording survives in Zhang Liao's biography: if Sun Quan arrives, Generals Zhang and Li sally out and fight, General Yue defends, and the protector does not join the battle. The biography records the generals' hesitation too, and Zhang Liao's answer to it — the master is far away, relief will not come in time, and the orders mean striking before the enemy is fully assembled.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao
06 / 16 In the sources
Li Dian's biography keeps both the feud and the morning it ended: his words are given closely — this is the state's business; may private wrong outweigh public duty? Chen Shou does not retell the killing itself; the grudge, and the decision to set it down, are what the record keeps.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 18, Biography of Li Dian
07 / 16 In the sources
The biography counts them: eight hundred men who dared death, gathered in one night. They were the edge of the garrison, not the garrison — picked so that one morning's work could be done before the enemy learned how few they were.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao
08 / 16 In the sources
The sortie is told blow by blow in Zhang Liao's biography: at dawn, armour on, halberd in hand, first into the line; dozens slain; two generals cut down; calling his own name as he charged to the foot of Sun Quan's banner. It is rare for the losing side's annals to keep the winner's account of a defeat; the Wu records do not contradict this one.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 67
09 / 16 In the sources
The scene stands in the victor's biography, and the Wu annals do not deny it: Sun Quan, taken by surprise, withdrew to high ground and guarded himself with a long halberd; challenged to come down, he did not dare. Then, seeing how few the riders were, he ordered them surrounded.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao; Wu shu 2, Annals of Sun Quan
10 / 16 In the sources
The double breaking of the ring is in the biography: the charge straight out, Sun Quan's men giving way before it so that none dared stand against him, then the cry of the enclosed men — General, will you abandon us? — and the return into the ring to bring the rest out. Sun Quan's army watched a few score riders do what an army could not.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao
11 / 16 In the sources
From dawn until noon, the biography says, and the spirit of the Wu men was taken. The sortie was never meant to win the war; it was meant to take the war's confidence, so that seven thousand could hold what a hundred thousand had expected to walk into.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao
12 / 16 In the sources
The Wu annals and the Zizhi Tongjian keep the same flat shape for the siege: more than ten days before Hefei, the city untaken, then withdrawal. No stratagem is recorded for the retreat and none needed — the histories simply let the army's exhaustion end the attempt.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 2, Annals of Sun Quan; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 67
13 / 16 In the sources
The Wu annals set the scene of the ambush themselves: the main body already withdrawn and on the road, Sun Quan with Ling Tong, Gan Ning and a few still north of the ford, when Zhang Liao's pursuit came down suddenly upon them. The ford gave the episode's second battle its name.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 2, Annals of Sun Quan; Wu shu 10, Biography of Ling Tong
14 / 16 Commentary
The escape is kept in Pei Songzhi's commentary to the Wu annals and adopted by the Zizhi Tongjian: the southern end of the bridge already removed, a zhang and more of gap remaining, and the close servant Gu Li whipping the horse from behind to help it clear the break. Sun Quan's survival was one horse-length long; the histories say so plainly.
Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Wu shu 2; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 67
15 / 16 In the sources
Ling Tong's biography pays the bill for the leap: his close troops to the last man, himself returning by swimming the ford in armour; Gan Ning drawing his bow against the pursuers. Ling Tong was in his middle twenties and dead of sickness within two years. The ford was not held; it was bought.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 10, Biographies of Ling Tong and Gan Ning
16 / 16 In the sources
Both details stand in Zhang Liao's biography: the inspection of the battlefield and the long sigh in 216, and the children of the south quieted with his name. A later edict of the Wei house fixed the arithmetic of the story — eight hundred against a hundred thousand; both figures remain the sources' claim, not a count.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao

Myth Check

The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a masterpiece — and not a source. Where its famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Eight hundred against a hundred thousand — the whole garrison against the whole army.

What the sources say

Both numbers are the sources' claims, not counts. A hundred thousand is the strength given for Sun Quan's army and repeated in a later Wei edict; eight hundred is only the picked sortie force, the edge of a garrison the Zizhi Tongjian puts at rather more than seven thousand. The arithmetic is a verdict, not a muster roll.

Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17 (with the Wei edict); Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 67
The story you may know

Hefei's three defenders all charged out of the gate together.

What the sources say

Cao Cao's sealed letter divided them: Generals Zhang and Li go out and fight, General Yue holds the city, and the protector takes no part in the battle. The sortie was Zhang Liao's and Li Dian's; Yue Jin's battle was the wall.

Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao
The story you may know

Sun Quan flew the broken bridge on a miracle horse.

What the sources say

The sources make it smaller and more human: the planks of the southern span had been pulled, a zhang and more of bridge still stood, and a servant named Gu Li whipped the horse from behind so it could clear the gap. The leap was paid for by Ling Tong's hundred men, who died on the north bank.

Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 67; Sanguozhi, Wu shu 10, Biography of Ling Tong
The story you may know

The story that Zhang Liao's name quieted crying children is a folk invention of later ages.

What the sources say

It is in the official history itself: Chen Shou closes the account of the battle by noting that in Wu, when children would not stop crying, their parents frightened them with Zhang Liao's name, and they were quiet.

Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang Liao
