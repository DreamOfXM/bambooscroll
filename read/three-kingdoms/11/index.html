The Gate and the Trackless Road

Chengdu, 263 CE — In the autumn of 263 three armies came down on the mountain kingdom of Shu-Han: one was stopped at a gate of sword-point cliffs, one vanished into seven hundred li of trackless mountains — and what came out on the other side ended a kingdom.

01 / 16 Note
Episode 11 is told, like the others, only from the official histories: the Records of the Three Kingdoms, Pei Songzhi's fifth-century commentary, the Book of Jin, and the Zizhi Tongjian. The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is used nowhere as a source; where it disagrees — on Jiang Wei's wars, on Jiangyou, on the character of the last emperor — the Myth Check at the end says so.
On our sources
02 / 16 In the sources
The design is in Zhong Hui's biography: he wished to take Shu and urged it on Sima Zhao, and warships were built first on the eastern rivers, openly as if against Wu, so that no one would be ready when the real edict came. In the autumn of Jingyuan 4 the three armies were named and sent.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Zhong Hui; Wei shu 4, Annals of the Three Lords, Jingyuan 4
03 / 16 In the sources
The order of battle stands in Zhong Hui's biography almost as a roster: Deng Ai and Zhuge Xu with somewhat more than thirty thousand each — Ai toward Gan Song and Tazhong to hold Jiang Wei fast, Xu toward Wujie and Qiaotou to cut off his way home — while Zhong Hui himself commanded rather more than a hundred thousand, entering from Xiegu and Luogu.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Zhong Hui; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 78
04 / 16 In the sources
The shelved warning is in Jiang Wei's biography: hearing that Zhong Hui was fitting out troops in Guanzhong, he asked that Liao Hua and Zhang Yi be sent to hold Yang'an Pass and the bridgehead at Yinping; Huang Hao, trusting ghosts and shamans, said the enemy would never come of themselves, the matter was put to sleep, and the ministers did not know. It was the quietest decision of the war.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 14, Biography of Jiang Wei
05 / 16 In the sources
The histories keep both the feint and the price of it: Jiang Wei turned as if to fall on Zhuge Xu's rear, Xu fell back, and Jiang Wei passed Qiaotou and made for Jiange. Zhong Hui then reported that Xu had failed to cut the enemy off; Xu was sent back in a caged cart, and his troops were merged into Zhong Hui's command.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Zhong Hui; Shu shu 14, Biography of Jiang Wei; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 78
06 / 16 In the sources
Zhong Hui's biography keeps the two gates and one tomb: Jiang Shu surrendered Yang'an Pass while Fu Qian died fighting; Han and Le fortresses held out and were bypassed; and Zhong Hui, passing Zhuge Liang's grave, offered sacrifice there and forbade his soldiers to cut wood or graze beasts near it. At Jiange he was stopped — the road narrow, the cliffs steep, the army's food failing — and withdrawal was discussed.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Zhong Hui; Shu shu 14, Biography of Jiang Wei
07 / 16 In the sources
The memorial is quoted nearly entire in Deng Ai's biography: from Yinping by the trackless way, through Han Deyang Pavilion, to Fu County, issuing west of Jiange and some three hundred li from Chengdu, striking the heart with an unexpected force. It closed on the words of the military books — attack where unprepared, appear where unexpected — and the promise: strike now at what is empty, and the break is certain.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Deng Ai
08 / 16 In the sources
The march is told in Deng Ai's biography in its hardest words: mountains high, valleys deep, hardship to the extreme; grain transport nearly spent, the force on the edge of ruin. Deng Ai wrapped himself in felt and pushed himself rolling down; officers and men climbed trees and scaled cliffs, advancing in single file like fish. What came out the far side was less an army than a remnant of one.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Deng Ai
09 / 16 In the sources
The Later Lord's annals give the fall of Jiangyou in a single clause: the garrison commander Ma Miao surrendered. The histories record no reason and no fight; from Jiangyou the road ran open toward Fu County and Chengdu, and the shock of it reached the capital.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 3, Biography of the Later Lord; Wei shu 28, Biography of Deng Ai
10 / 16 In the sources
The hesitation is in Zhuge Zhan's biography: he led the army out and halted at Fu County without advancing, while Huang Chong urged him again and again to take the defiles quickly and not let the enemy reach level ground; Zhan could not act. Deng Ai's troops took the defiles, and Zhan fell back to Mianzhu and stood to fight. Deng Ai first sent a letter tempting him — surrender, and he would be recommended as King of Langye — and Zhan, in anger, killed the envoy.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 5, Biography of Zhuge Zhan; Shu shu 13, Biography of Huang Chong
11 / 16 In the sources
The battle is told from the winning side in Deng Ai's biography: the first attacks by Deng Zhong and Sima Shi beaten back, both returning to say the enemy could not be struck; Deng Ai's fury — the day of life or death, the difference between victory and defeat, stands in this one battle — and his threat to behead them; the renewed charge, and Zhuge Zhan's great defeat, with several thousand dead upon the field.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Deng Ai
12 / 16 In the sources
The deaths stand in the biographies: Zhuge Zhan died in the line; Zhuge Shang, with the sigh about Huang Hao, spurred into the Wei army and died; Zhang Zun and Huang Chong died in the same battle — a grandson of Zhang Fei and a son of Huang Quan, the houses of the founders dying with the house of Zhuge Liang. After Mianzhu, Deng Ai marched on Chengdu.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 5, Biography of Zhuge Zhan; Shu shu 6, Biography of Zhang Zun; Shu shu 13, Biography of Huang Chong
13 / 16 In the sources
The surrender scene is in the Later Lord's annals: Deng Ai reached the north of the city; the Later Lord came out carrying a coffin and bound himself, asking to surrender; Deng Ai released the bonds and burned the coffin, and invited him to an audience. Qiao Zhou's biography keeps the debate that went before — the counsels to flee south or east, and his argument that only surrender to Wei had a certain outcome. The kingdom Liu Bei founded ended at an open gate, forty years after his death.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 3, Biography of the Later Lord; Shu shu 12, Biography of Qiao Zhou
14 / 16 In the sources
The rage at Jiange is in Jiang Wei's biography: officers and soldiers all furious, drawing their swords and hacking at the rocks. Jiang Wei surrendered to Zhong Hui, who restored their seals and batons and treated them generously, riding in the same carriage and sitting on the same mat; Zhong Hui said that measured against the famous men of the Central Plains, Zhuge Dan and Xiahou Xuan could not surpass him. What Zhong Hui meant to do with that friendship, the first month of 264 showed.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 14, Biography of Jiang Wei; Wei shu 28, Biography of Zhong Hui
15 / 16 Commentary
The feast is kept by the Han Jin Chunqiu, quoted into the Later Lord's annals by Pei Songzhi: the Shu music, the sorrow of the old Shu ministers, Liu Shan laughing and drinking as usual; his words — I am happy here, I do not miss Shu — then Xi Zheng's coaching and the second, admitted answer. The record keeps his words without judging them: a fool, or a prisoner who knew a captive must not be seen missing his kingdom.
Han Jin Chunqiu, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Shu shu 3
16 / 16 Debated
The rebellion and the deaths are in Zhong Hui's biography and Jiang Wei's. What Jiang Wei meant by them is less certain: a memorial to Liu Shan survives — bear, Your Majesty, the humiliation of a few days; I am attempting to turn peril into safety — but whether he truly plotted to restore Shu, or only threw himself into the conqueror's quarrel to survive, the sources do not settle, and readers from early on took sides.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28, Biography of Zhong Hui; Shu shu 14, Biography of Jiang Wei, with Pei Songzhi's commentary

In this episode

The people who carried this year. The sources disagree about almost everything else.

Deng Ai鄧艾
d. 264
Frontier general who rose out of poverty. Held the western border against Jiang Wei for a decade, then took some thirty thousand men over seven hundred li of trackless mountains, broke Zhuge Zhan at Mianzhu, and received Chengdu's surrender. He was dead within months, killed in the aftermath of his own victory.
Zhong Hui鍾會
225–264
Son of the calligrapher Zhong You, and the man who planned the conquest of Shu. Led more than a hundred thousand to Jiange and could not pass it; watched Deng Ai win the war; then raised rebellion at Chengdu and died in the mutiny, aged forty.
Jiang Wei姜維
202–264
Shu-Han's last great general, twenty-five years at war with the north. Held Zhong Hui's main army at Jiange, took his own emperor's order to surrender, and then tried to turn the conquerors against each other; died in the mutiny at Chengdu in 264.
Liu Shan劉禪
207–271
Liu Bei's son, forty-one years emperor of Shu-Han. Surrendered Chengdu in 263 to spare it a sack; at Luoyang told Sima Zhao he was content and did not miss Shu. The record keeps his words and leaves the judgment to the reader.
Zhuge Zhan諸葛瞻
227–263
Zhuge Liang's son and Shu's last field commander. Refused Deng Ai's offer of a kingship and killed the messenger, then died with his son at Mianzhu in 263, aged thirty-seven.

Myth Check

The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a masterpiece — and not a source. Where its famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Jiang Wei launched nine campaigns into the Central Plains — the novel's famous number.

What the sources say

The number nine belongs to the Romance; the record gives the campaigns one by one, and counted they come to about eleven over some twenty-five years (238–262 CE). Almost all of them were aimed at Longyou, Wei's western frontier — not the Central Plains — and most were small affairs. The one great victory was Taoxi in 255; the last, in 262, was the defeat that sent Jiang Wei to farm at Tazhong, where the war of 263 found him.

Sanguozhi, Shu shu 14, Biography of Jiang Wei
The story you may know

Liu Shan was a pure imbecile — the proverbial A-Dou who could not be set upright.

What the sources say

He reigned forty-one years, and Chen Shou's appraisal says that with a worthy chancellor he was a reasonable ruler. His surrender was a reasoned decision taken on Qiao Zhou's recorded argument that it would spare Chengdu a sack. And at Luoyang, his words about being happy may be read as the caution of a captive whose life depended on missing nothing — the sources keep both readings open.

Sanguozhi, Shu shu 3, with Chen Shou's appraisal; Shu shu 12, Biography of Qiao Zhou
The story you may know

Shu-Han fell at a push, and Chengdu surrendered to Zhong Hui's great army.

What the sources say

The main army took nothing: Zhong Hui's hundred-thousand-plus stood helplessly at Jiange against Jiang Wei to the end, and Yang'an Pass had cost Fu Qian his life. Zhuge Zhan fought and died at Mianzhu with his son, a grandson of Zhang Fei and a son of Huang Quan. Chengdu surrendered to the remnant that came down out of Yinping, not to the main blade.

Sanguozhi, Wei shu 28; Shu shu 5; Shu shu 14
The story you may know

At Jiangyou, Ma Miao's wife Lady Li shamed him into dying for Shu — a noble couple in the novel's pages.

What the sources say

The record gives the whole affair in one clause: the garrison commander Ma Miao surrendered. Lady Li and her death of remonstrance are inventions of the Romance, written centuries after the event.

Sanguozhi, Shu shu 3, Biography of the Later Lord (Lady Li appears only in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms)

Sources

Every caption and quotation in this episode traces to one of these. Where scholars disagree, the panel says so.

  • Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi) — Chen Shou c. 280s CE Shu shu 3 (the Later Lord), Shu shu 5 (Zhuge Liang and Zhuge Zhan), Shu shu 12 (Qiao Zhou), Shu shu 13 (Huang Chong), Shu shu 14 (Jiang Wei), Wei shu 28 (Deng Ai and Zhong Hui): the suppressed warning, Jiange, Yinping, Mianzhu, and the surrender.
  • Commentary to the Sanguozhi — Pei Songzhi 429 CE Preserves the Han Jin Chunqiu's account of the feast at Luoyang — Liu Shan's 'I am happy here; I do not miss Shu' — and keeps the doubts raised early on about Jiang Wei's last memorial.
  • Book of Jin (Jin shu) — Fang Xuanling et al. 648 CE Annals of Emperor Wen (Sima Zhao) and the biography of Wei Guan: the conquest from the Wei-Jin side, and the aftermath in which Wei Guan had the victorious Deng Ai arrested and killed.
  • Zizhi Tongjian — Sima Guang 1084 CE Scroll 78, Jingyuan 4 and Xianxi 1 (263–264 CE): the campaign in sequence — the three armies, Qiaotou, Jiange, the crossing of Yinping, Mianzhu, the surrender, and the mutiny at Chengdu.
  • Han Jin Chunqiu — Sun Sheng 4th c. CE The source, inside Pei Songzhi's commentary, for the Luoyang banquet: the Shu music and dancers, the weeping ministers, and the last emperor's laugh.
