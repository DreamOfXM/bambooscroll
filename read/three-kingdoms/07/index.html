The Heights Above the River

Hanzhong, 217–219 CE — Two years, one river crossing, and one charge downhill with the drums rolling — the campaign that took Hanzhong from Cao Cao was won by an old general and planned by a strategist the novel gave to someone else.

01 / 16 Note
Episode 7 is told, like the others, only from the official histories: the Records of the Three Kingdoms, Pei Songzhi's fifth-century commentary, and the Zizhi Tongjian. The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is used nowhere as a source; where it disagrees, the Myth Check at the end says so.
On our sources
02 / 16 In the sources
Huang Quan's warning stands in his biography, spoken when Cao Cao took Hanzhong in 215 and left generals to hold it instead of pushing south into Shu. Two years later the warning became the war plan.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 13, Biography of Huang Quan; Shu shu 2, Biography of the Former Lord
03 / 16 In the sources
Fa Zheng's biography keeps the persuasion with its three courses, and that Liu Bei adopted the plan and Fa Zheng went with the army. Zhuge Liang's biography gives his office through these years: whenever the Former Lord went out, Zhuge Liang stayed to hold Chengdu, food and troops enough. The Hanzhong campaign was planned at the front, not at Chengdu.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 7, Biography of Fa Zheng; Shu shu 5, Biography of Zhuge Liang
04 / 16 In the sources
The Xiabian expedition is recorded as a defeat: Cao Hong broke Wu Lan, Zhang Fei and Ma Chao had to turn back, and Lei Tong died. The main march went forward all the same.
Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 1, Annals of Emperor Wu
05 / 16 In the sources
The Former Lord's biography gives the stalemate in one phrase: he camped at Yangping Pass and faced Xiahou Yuan. Yuan had held Hanzhong for Cao Cao since 215, his reward for the western wars the title General Who Attacks the West.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 2, Biography of the Former Lord; Wei shu 9, Biography of Xiahou Yuan
06 / 16 In the sources
Zhang He's biography keeps the night: ten thousand picked troops against his camp, and He fighting at the head of his bodyguards until Liu Bei could not take it. It was the last Wei success of the campaign.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang He; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
07 / 16 In the sources
The crossing is in the Former Lord's biography almost as an itinerary: south from Yangping, over the Mian, up the mountain by degrees, camps made on the heights. Wei's account adds what it did to the defenders: Yuan led his troops to contest the ground and could not dislodge them.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 2, Biography of the Former Lord; Wei shu 9, Biography of Xiahou Yuan
08 / 16 In the sources
Wei's annals give the mechanics: the stakes burned, Yuan dividing his troops to help Zhang He, and the attack falling where he was thin. The Shu side credited the design to Fa Zheng; the victory at Dingjun was counted his.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Xiahou Yuan; Shu shu 2; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
09 / 16 In the sources
Huang Zhong's biography records the day: he pushed his vanguard forward with drums rolling, Yuan's army was badly defeated, and Yuan was beheaded together with Zhao Yong, whom Cao Cao had made Inspector of Yi Province. For that one day Huang Zhong was raised to General Who Attacks the West.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6, Biography of Huang Zhong
10 / 16 In the sources
In the confusion after Yuan's death, Guo Huai and Du Xi urged that Zhang He take provisional command, and the army steadied; Cao Cao confirmed the choice by remote appointment. What was left of the Wei line now stood at Yangping Pass.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Xiahou Yuan; Wei shu 17, Biography of Zhang He; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
11 / 16 In the sources
The relief march is in the Wei annals: the king himself out of Chang'an by the Xie Valley, bound for Yangping. The Former Lord's biography keeps the words Liu Bei said on the heights — translated closely above — and the decision that went with them.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 1, Annals of Emperor Wu; Shu shu 2, Biography of the Former Lord; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
12 / 16 Debated
The accounts of the retreat divide: the Shu annals credit Liu Bei's refusal to fight and steady desertion in Cao Cao's camps; the chicken-rib anecdote preserved in commentary makes the withdrawal a private decision. The sources do not weigh one against the other, and neither army's losses were ever counted.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 2; Wei shu 1; Cao Man Zhuan, in Pei Songzhi's commentary
13 / 16 Commentary
The anecdote comes from the Cao Man Zhuan, quoted by Pei Songzhi; the annals themselves do not tell it. The Zizhi Tongjian admitted it into the narrative, and later tellings have often let it stand for the whole reason of the retreat.
Cao Man Zhuan, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Wei shu 1
14 / 16 Commentary
The open gate, the drums and crossbows, and the praise are in the Yun Bie Zhuan, a separate memoir appended by Pei Songzhi to Zhao Yun's biography — it stands here as commentary, not as Chen Shou's main text.
Yun Bie Zhuan, in Pei Songzhi's commentary to Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6
15 / 16 In the sources
Both annals agree on the ending: in the fifth month Cao Cao withdrew his army and returned to Chang'an. What the Shu annals add is the prize — Hanzhong, and with it the whole northern gate of Shu.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 2, Biography of the Former Lord; Wei shu 1, Annals of Emperor Wu
16 / 16 In the sources
The proclamation stands in the Former Lord's biography. Fa Zheng's biography records his offices in the new kingdom and his death the following year, for which Liu Bei wept many days.
Sanguozhi, Shu shu 2, Biography of the Former Lord; Shu shu 7, Biography of Fa Zheng

The people who carried this year. The sources disagree about almost everything else.

Liu Bei劉備
161–223 CE
Claimant of the house of Han in Shu. Marched north for the gate that shielded his province, held the heights against Cao Cao himself, and took a king's title in Hanzhong.
Fa Zheng法正
176–220 CE
Chief strategist of the campaign. Argued for the march on Hanzhong, planned the stroke at Dingjun, and became Prefect of the Masters of Writing in the new kingdom; died the year after the victory.
Huang Zhong黃忠
d. 220 CE
Liu Bei's veteran general. Charged down Mount Dingjun with the drums rolling in 219, broke Xiahou Yuan's army, and slew him.
Xiahou Yuan夏侯淵
d. 219 CE
Cao Cao's general in the west, of whom his soldiers said five hundred li in three days. Held Hanzhong for four years; died beneath the heights of Dingjun.
Cao Cao曹操
155–220 CE
Master of the north. Left two generals to hold Hanzhong for him, came in person to save it, and went home without a battle.

Myth Check

The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a masterpiece — and not a source. Where its famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

The Hanzhong campaign was masterminded by Zhuge Liang.

What the sources say

The histories name Fa Zheng: the persuasion to march, the three-course plan, and the design at Dingjun were his, and he went with the army. Zhuge Liang's recorded office through these years was holding Chengdu, feeding the army and supplying the troops. The novel has Zhuge Liang directing the whole war by letter from Chengdu; no history supports it.

Romance of the Three Kingdoms, chs. 70–73; corrected by Sanguozhi, Shu shu 2, Shu shu 5 and Shu shu 7; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 68
The story you may know

Huang Zhong slew Xiahou Yuan in single combat, as in the opera Dingjun Mountain.

What the sources say

No duel is recorded. The histories give an assault: Huang Zhong charged down from the heights with drums rolling, Yuan's army was badly defeated, and Yuan was killed in the rout together with Zhao Yong. The duel, the exchange of prisoners, and the opera's other business are later invention.

Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 71, and the opera Dingjun Shan; absent from Sanguozhi, Shu shu 6 and Wei shu 9
The story you may know

Cao Cao lost Hanzhong in a great pitched battle.

What the sources say

After Dingjun, Cao Cao came in person with a relief army and the war ended almost without fighting: Liu Bei held the heights and refused battle, desertions in Cao Cao's camps grew daily, and in the fifth month the northern army withdrew.

Sanguozhi, Shu shu 2, Biography of the Former Lord; Wei shu 1, Annals of Emperor Wu
The story you may know

The campaign turned on a string of duels — Zhang He against Huang Zhong, month after month of single combat.

What the sources say

The histories record two engagements: a failed night attack on Zhang He's camp at Guangshi, and the descent from Dingjun. No single combat between generals is recorded anywhere in the campaign; the war was decided by ground — who held the heights.

Romance of the Three Kingdoms, chs. 70–71; Sanguozhi, Wei shu 17 and Shu shu 6
