The Fall of Wu

The Yangtze, 280 CE — Seven years of ships, one river of iron, and a last emperor who came to the camp gate with his hands bound — in the spring of 280 the Yangtze carried an empire down to the sea, and the Three Kingdoms ended.

01 / 16 Note
Episode 12 is told, like the others, only from the official histories: the Book of Jin, the Records of the Three Kingdoms, and the Zizhi Tongjian. The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is used nowhere as a source; the line that opens this episode is a Tang poem written five centuries after the event, and where the novel tidied the ending, the Myth Check at the close says so.
On our sources
02 / 16 In the sources
Yang Hu's biography keeps the design: he kept Wang Jun in Yi Province and had ships built there in secret, so that the war was prepared at the headwaters where Wu could not touch it. When Yang Hu died in 278 he named Du Yu as his successor, and Du Yu kept the plan alive at Xiangyang. The invasion of 280 was fought with a fleet seven years old.
Jin shu, ch. 34, Biography of Yang Hu
03 / 16 In the sources
Wang Jun's biography gives the measure of the fleet: great linked ships, the largest a hundred and twenty paces square, holding over two thousand men, with towers and parapets raised on them where horses could be galloped — and the shavings of the yards thrown into the river, covering the water as they drifted downstream. The shavings matter: they were how Wu first heard of the fleet.
Jin shu, ch. 42, Biography of Wang Jun
04 / 16 In the sources
The warning stands in the same biography: seeing the shavings drift past his post, Wu Yan said that Jin must surely be planning an attack — and Sun Hao did not act on it. When the fleet came down, Jianping was not strong enough to hold it.
Jin shu, ch. 42, Biography of Wang Jun
05 / 16 In the sources
Sun Hao's annals keep the record of his rule — the flaying of faces, the gouging of eyes, the killings on suspicion — and the same charges surface again at his surrender banquet in Luoyang, when Jia Chong taunted him with them. The state that met Jin in 280 was not conquered from outside first; it had rotted from within.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 3, Annals of Sun Hao; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
06 / 16 In the sources
The scene is kept in Du Yu's biography: Emperor Wu was playing go with the Zhongshu-ling Zhang Hua when Du Yu's memorial arrived, and Zhang Hua pushed the board aside and folded his hands — Your Majesty is sage, martial and divine, the state rich and strong, the ruler of Wu licentious and cruel; strike now and Wu can be pacified in one stroke. Much of the court urged delay to the end; the decision went against them.
Jin shu, ch. 34, Biography of Du Yu
07 / 16 In the sources
The disposition survives in the Annals of Emperor Wu, with the generals named and their axes of advance, east and west together rather more than two hundred thousand. That figure is the edict's arithmetic, not a muster; what it records is the shape of the war — pressure applied to every reach of the river at once, so that Wu could move troops from no front to any other.
Jin shu, ch. 3, Annals of Emperor Wu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
08 / 16 Debated
Wang Jun's biography records the defences — the Wu stretched iron chains across the river, a thousand fathoms long, and additionally made iron spikes more than a zhang long, sunk secretly in the channel to block ships — but it does not record the place. The sources never say at which narrows the chains were drawn, and later readers have set them at different points of the middle reaches; the defences are certain, their exact map is not.
Jin shu, ch. 42, Biography of Wang Jun
09 / 16 In the sources
The clearing of the river is told step by step in the biography: the great rafts and the straw men, the spikes catching on the rafts and being dragged away, the torches more than ten zhang long and dozens of arms around, soaked in sesame oil, and the chains melting and breaking in a short while. In the second month the fleet took Xiling, Jingmen, Yidao and Lexiang one after another; nothing on the upper river stood between it and Jianye.
Jin shu, ch. 42, Biography of Wang Jun
10 / 16 In the sources
Jiangling's fall is in Du Yu's biography: Wu Yan, the Wu commander there, feigned surrender and put his troops on the walls, and Du Yu attacked and took the city; the Zizhi Tongjian dates the taking to the jiawu day of the first month. The bamboo image is Du Yu's own, spoken to generals who wanted to pause, and it became the proverb of the campaign.
Jin shu, ch. 34, Biography of Du Yu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
11 / 16 In the sources
The last field battle is kept in the Zizhi Tongjian: Zhang Ti led thirty thousand across the river to give battle, was greatly defeated at Banqiao, and died; his own words are preserved — if we are defeated, we die together with the altars of state, and there is no further regret. The Jin shu adds the arithmetic of the victory dispatches: rather more than two thousand were actually killed, while Wang Hun and Wang Jun claimed them by the ten thousand.
Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81; Jin shu, ch. 42, Biography of Wang Hun
12 / 16 In the sources
Sun Hao's annals keep the end from the inside: hearing that the Jin troops had entered in force, Tao Jun led his troops back east — and that night the whole army fled and melted away. No stratagem is recorded and no treachery; the sources keep only the disappearance of the will to fight.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 3, Annals of Sun Hao; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
13 / 16 In the sources
The surrender is dated in the Annals of Emperor Wu to the renyin day of the third month: Sun Hao, with his hands bound and a coffin borne, surrendered at the army's gate; the bindings were loosed and the coffin burned. To whom the surrender belonged was disputed almost at once — Wang Jun received it at Shitou, and Wang Hun's anger at that fed the quarrel that followed.
Jin shu, ch. 3, Annals of Emperor Wu; Jin shu, ch. 42, Biography of Wang Jun; Sanguozhi, Wu shu 3
14 / 16 Debated
The dispute is kept in full in Wang Jun's biography: Wang Hun's accusation of disobedience, Wang Jun's grievance, and the emperor's tolerance of both. Fan Tong's counsel to Wang Jun — do not speak of the matter of pacifying Wu — records the court's real verdict: the question of who deserved what could not be answered cleanly, and the sources keep the quarrel rather than settle it.
Jin shu, ch. 42, Biographies of Wang Hun and Wang Jun; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
15 / 16 In the sources
The banquet and its exchanges are in the Zizhi Tongjian: the emperor's seat, Sun Hao's retort, and Jia Chong's taunt about the flaying and eye-gouging, answered with the penalty for a subject who murders his lord. Sun Hao was enfeoffed the Marquis of Guiming; he died at Luoyang in 284, and the annals of Wu close with his letter of surrender — the mandate of Wu, it says, is ended.
Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81; Sanguozhi, Wu shu 3
16 / 16 In the sources
The official chronology closes the Three Kingdoms at 280: Shu had fallen in 263, Wei yielded its throne in 265, and Wu alone remained. Its conquest left one empire under Jin — which the Jin shu itself shows cracking within a decade, as the war of the princes broke the peace that 280 made.
Jin shu, ch. 3, Annals of Emperor Wu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81

Myth Check

The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a masterpiece — and not a source. Where its famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

The novel opens with 'empires long divided must unite, long united must divide' — and closes the fall of Wu as destiny's tidy moral arc, the last page of one clean story.

What the sources say

The record shows a planned conquest: seven years of shipbuilding, six columns of over two hundred thousand men, and a Jin court divided over whether to launch at all — all aimed at a state that had already lost the will of its own officials. And the unity was not the moral the novel implies: within a decade the house of Jin was at war with itself, and the peace of 280 did not last a generation.

Jin shu, ch. 3; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
The story you may know

Wu fell in one great last battle on the river, with its emperor dying in the fight.

What the sources say

The last field army was destroyed at Banqiao in the third month; Tao Jun's relief force melted away in the night without striking a blow; and Sun Hao came to the army's gate with his hands bound and a coffin, was sent to Luoyang as the Marquis of Guiming, and died there in 284. The end was a surrender, not a last stand.

Sanguozhi, Wu shu 3; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
The story you may know

Wang Jun's fleet fought its way down the whole river and won the war alone.

What the sources say

The invasion was six columns: Du Yu took Jiangling by assault and momentum in the first month, Wang Hun destroyed Zhang Ti's field army at Banqiao, and the fleet broke the middle-river defences in the second month and reached Shitou after the defending army had ceased to exist. The victory was a system, not a flotilla.

Jin shu, ch. 3, ch. 34, ch. 42
The story you may know

The exchange at the surrender banquet — Sima Yan's 'I have kept this seat for you a long time' and Sun Hao's 'your servant too set up a seat in the south to await Your Majesty' — is the novelist's flourish to give the fallen tyrant one last spark of face.

What the sources say

It is in the record: the Zizhi Tongjian keeps the whole banquet, including Jia Chong's taunt about the flaying and eye-gouging and Sun Hao's answer that such is the penalty for subjects who murder their lord. The novel needed to invent nothing here; the histories had kept it all along.

Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 81
