The Bait at Shiting

Shiting, 228 CE — A Wu official offered Cao Xiu his head of hair as the price of treason; a hundred thousand Wei soldiers followed the bait into the mountains, and Lu Xun was waiting at the end of the road.
16 panels~6 min read free to readevery line sourced
01 / 16 Note
Episode 9 is told, like the others, only from the official histories: the Records of the Three Kingdoms, Pei Songzhi's fifth-century commentary, and the Zizhi Tongjian. The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is used nowhere as a source — and Shiting, one of the largest victories the Wu record gives, is exactly the kind of battle the novel pushes to the edge of the page. The Myth Check at the end says where.
On our sources
02 / 16 In the sources
The three-road offensive is in the Wei annals and Cao Xiu's biography, and the Zizhi Tongjian sets the sequence under 228: Cao Xiu against Poyang, Sima Yi toward Jiangling, Cao Zhen against Zhongkou. Chen Shou, who served in the Jin court that succeeded Wei, records the plan without excuse-making; the defeat that followed is told from the Wei side itself.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 3, Annals of Ming Di; Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Xiu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
03 / 16 In the sources
Zhou Fang's biography records that he repeatedly sent letters to Cao Xiu enticing him to attack, and that the letters were in seven articles — seven counts of inducement. The biography also keeps the shape of the deception: the letters were written with Sun Quan's knowledge, and Zhou Fang's agents were sent at the king's direction. This was not a rogue prefect; it was state policy wearing one man's face.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 12, Biography of Zhou Fang
04 / 16 In the sources
Zhou Fang's biography keeps the scene: the northern spies demanded a token of sincerity, and Zhou Fang cut off his hair — an act the sources themselves call grave, since the body and its hair are received from one's parents and not to be mutilated. It is one of the few stratagems of the period where the price is recorded along with the trick.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 12, Biography of Zhou Fang; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
05 / 16 In the sources
Lu Xun's biography records that in the third year of Huangwu, when Cao Xiu came with a hundred thousand toward Poyang, Sun Quan made Lu Xun grand commander and gave him the golden tally, commanding the six armies and all officers in the campaign. Sun Quan's own words here are not in the record; they are this episode's rendering of the charge the sources describe.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 13, Biography of Lu Xun; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
06 / 16 Debated
The strength the Wei sources give is a hundred thousand; Lu Xun's biography repeats it when it says Cao Xiu came with a hundred thousand men toward Poyang. Like a hundred thousand at Hefei thirteen years before, it is a claim, not a count: round numbers of this size mark the size of the risk. This episode treats it so throughout.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 13, Biography of Lu Xun; Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Xiu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
07 / 16 In the sources
Cao Xiu's biography describes the route: he led the army toward Poyang and met Zhou Fang's forces, and when the fighting began he withdrew and his baggage train was lost on the Jiashi road. The narrow defile country between the Yangtze and the Dabie foothills is what made the bait work; a hundred thousand men on one causeway is not a hundred thousand men in a battle line.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Xiu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
08 / 16 In the sources
The record gives the shape of the discovery without the scene: Zhou Fang's biography says he closed his gates and held his commandery when the Wei army came, and the Wu annals say Cao Xiu, discovering the deception, was already too deep to withdraw in good order. What the generals saw that morning is this episode's reconstruction from those two facts.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 12, Biography of Zhou Fang; Wu shu 2, Annals of Sun Quan
09 / 16 In the sources
Lu Xun's biography gives the disposition: he was grand commander over the six armies, with Quan Cong as a wing, and it was in the hills that the Wei army was engaged and broken. Lu Xun's words here are the episode's own; the sources keep only the fact that the trap was sprung with the Wei army already committed to the defile.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 13, Biography of Lu Xun
10 / 16 In the sources
The Wu annals give the tally: of Cao Xiu's army, ten thousand were killed or captured, and the whole baggage train and equipment were taken. Lu Xun's biography says the Wei army was routed and Cao Xiu barely escaped with his life. Casualty figures of this kind are the winner's count; the escape of the commander is confirmed by the loser's own records.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 2, Annals of Sun Quan; Wu shu 13, Biography of Lu Xun
11 / 16 In the sources
The retreat is in the Wei biography itself: Cao Xiu withdrew along the Jiashi road and the army's equipment and stores were all lost. The Zizhi Tongjian keeps the same shape and adds the pursuit as far as Jiakou. That the victor's tally and the loser's annals agree on the loss of the baggage train is as close to proof as these records come.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Xiu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
12 / 16 In the sources
Jia Kui's biography records that his timely arrival saved Cao Xiu's remaining forces; the Wu army, meeting Jia Kui's fresh column, withdrew from the pursuit. The Wei records also keep what Jia Kui had said before the march — that the deep advance was dangerous and that Zhou Fang's defection should not be trusted.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 15, Biography of Jia Kui; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
13 / 16 In the sources
The Wu annals record the victory tally and Sun Quan's praise of Zhou Fang, holding his deed up before the assembled officers. Sun Quan's words here are paraphrase; what the record keeps is that the king made the haircut the public measure of the victory, and that Zhou Fang's family was raised in honour for it.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 12, Biography of Zhou Fang; Wu shu 2, Annals of Sun Quan
14 / 16 In the sources
Cao Xiu's biography records that after the defeat he was recalled and died within the year, of an illness — the sources say an ailment of the back. Later Wei accounts connect the death to the shame of the defeat; the primary record keeps both the illness and the defeat, and leaves the reader to join them.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Xiu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
15 / 16 In the sources
Jia Kui's biography records his death in the same year as Shiting, and keeps his final counsel: that the eastern frontier be held by steady defence rather than deep campaigns. With Cao Xiu dead of illness the same year, Wei's eastern offensive leadership died twice over in 228 — the price of one Wu stratagem.
Sanguozhi, Wei shu 15, Biography of Jia Kui
16 / 16 Note
The balance of the sources is the point: the Wu annals and Lu Xun's biography treat Shiting as one of the two great defensive victories of the house of Sun, with Yiling; the Wei records keep the defeat and the deaths of both eastern commanders in the same year. The Romance of the Three Kingdoms, by contrast, passes over it in a few paragraphs, because it is a Wu victory that belongs to no famous hero of the novel's cast.
Sanguozhi, Wu shu 2 and Wu shu 13; Wei shu 9 and Wei shu 15; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71

Myth Check

The novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a masterpiece — and not a source. Where its famous scenes depart from the histories, we leave them out of the story and put them here.

The story you may know

Shiting is a side-note of the Three Kingdoms period — a skirmish the sources barely mention.

What the sources say

The official histories treat it as one of the largest set-piece victories of the whole Wu record: a Wei army given as a hundred thousand strong was lured into the hills and routed, its baggage train taken entire, and both Wei commanders of the eastern front died within the year. It is the novel, not the record, that shrinks it.

Sanguozhi, Wu shu 2 (Annals of Sun Quan); Wu shu 13 (Lu Xun); Wei shu 9 (Cao Xiu); Wei shu 15 (Jia Kui)
The story you may know

Zhou Fang cut his hair to Cao Xiu himself, in the Wei camp, as a defector's oath.

What the sources say

The sources put the haircut at home, before his own king's court, as the token the northern spies demanded — and they mark it as grave self-mutilation, since the hair is received from one's parents. It was a price paid inside Wu, not an oath sworn inside Wei.

Sanguozhi, Wu shu 12, Biography of Zhou Fang; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
The story you may know

Cao Xiu was killed in the battle, or died on the field of his wounds.

What the sources say

The record is quieter and colder: Cao Xiu escaped the rout, was recalled, and died of illness — an ailment of the back — later the same year. The Wei sources connect the death to the shame of the defeat, but they do not say the battle killed him.

Sanguozhi, Wei shu 9, Biography of Cao Xiu; Zizhi Tongjian, scroll 71
The story you may know

Lu Xun won Shiting alone, by the same kind of single stroke of genius the novel gives him at Yiling.

What the sources say

The sources give a campaign with three authors: Zhou Fang, who spent years feeding Cao Xiu false intelligence; Sun Quan, who authorised the deception and the command; and Lu Xun, who held the trap and sprang it. It was statecraft, not sorcery — and the novel, which prizes the lone genius, mostly leaves this one out.

Sanguozhi, Wu shu 12 (Zhou Fang); Wu shu 13 (Lu Xun); Wu shu 2 (Annals of Sun Quan)
